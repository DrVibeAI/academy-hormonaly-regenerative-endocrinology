## Lesson 2.1 · Cortisol signatures: local 11β-HSD1 and Cushing's syndrome

### 01 / 19 · The face that might be a diagnosis.  
*Module 02 · Hormones written on the skin · title*

Most skin changes you see are ageing or lifestyle. A few are the first visible sign of endocrine disease. In three lessons you will separate them: what local and systemic hormones do to skin, what hormone therapy can and cannot do for it, and which signs belong with an endocrinologist.

- 2.1 Cortisol on the skin: local 11β-HSD1 and Cushing's syndrome
- 2.2 Estrogen, menopause and collagen: what the trials show
- 2.3 Skin signs that need an endocrinologist

### 02 / 19 · The skin regenerates its own cortisol.  
*Mechanism · audio*

Skin expresses the machinery of a local stress axis, including CRH and POMC with its product ACTH. Keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts also carry 11β-HSD1, the enzyme that turns inactive cortisone back into active cortisol inside the cell. In human skin samples its activity is higher with age and in sun-exposed skin, and local cortisol availability is set independently of blood levels. Cortisol slows collagen production in dermal fibroblasts, and mice lacking the enzyme kept their dermal thickness with age.

- Local axis: CRH, POMC and ACTH are made in skin
- 11β-HSD1 regenerates cortisol inside skin cells
- Mechanism register: human samples, cultured cells and mice

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Picture the skin as a small endocrine organ with its own stress axis. Skin cells make CRH and ACTH, the same signals that drive the adrenal glands, and they carry the receptors to respond to them. They also carry an enzyme called eleven beta HSD one, which turns inactive cortisone back into active cortisol inside the cell. In human skin samples that enzyme works harder with age and in sun-exposed skin, and it sets local cortisol independently of the level in the blood. Cortisol slows collagen production in dermal fibroblasts, and mice bred without the enzyme kept their dermal thickness as they aged. Hold all of this in the mechanism register. It comes from biopsies, cultured cells and mice, and it says nothing yet about how a patient's face will age.

</details>

### 03 / 19 · 11β-HSD1: four separate answers.  
*Four registers · tool*

Local cortisol regeneration is a real and interesting mechanism. Each register below answers a different question, and blending them produces the line you will hear online: that blocking cortisol in the skin reverses ageing.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Plausible | Enzyme activity rises with age and sun exposure in human skin samples; knockout mice resist age-related dermal thinning |
| Human evidence | One pilot RCT · GRADE C | 28 adults with type 2 diabetes took an oral inhibitor: the primary outcome was missed, wounds closed faster in an exploratory analysis, and barrier function was modestly worse |
| Regulatory status (US, 24 September 2026) | Investigational only | The inhibitor tested in human skin is a research compound with no FDA approval |
| Open question | Does it drive visible ageing? | No registered trial tests an 11β-HSD1 inhibitor for skin ageing (ClinicalTrials.gov, 24 September 2026) |

**Callout:** Local cortisol is set inside the skin, independently of blood levels, so a systemic cortisol test does not measure it.

### 04 / 19 · Which features point to Cushing's?  
*Sort the signs · tool*

Cushing's syndrome shares most of its features with ordinary weight gain, poor sleep and stress. The Endocrine Society's diagnostic guideline separates the few that discriminate from the many that are common. Before you see its verdicts, sort these eight.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Easy bruising | Skin | Bruises without an obvious injury |
| Facial plethora | Face | A persistently red, full-blooded complexion |
| Proximal muscle weakness | History and examination | Trouble rising from a chair or climbing stairs |
| Striae | Abdomen, flanks, thighs | Reddish-purple and wider than 1 cm |
| Facial fullness | Face | A rounder face than a year ago |
| Weight gain | History | Gradual gain over several years |
| Acne or hirsutism | Skin | New or persistent |
| Fatigue and poor sleep | History | Tired, sleeping badly |

**Callout:** Few features of Cushing's syndrome are unique. Some discriminate far better than others.

**Exercise (classify) · Discriminating or common?** — Tap where each feature belongs.
- Easy bruising → **Best discriminates Cushing's** — *One of the four features the guideline lists as best discriminating, particularly bruising with no obvious trauma.*
- Facial plethora → **Best discriminates Cushing's** — *Listed among the best discriminators. Most of the four have low sensitivity, so their absence does not exclude the syndrome.*
- Proximal muscle weakness → **Best discriminates Cushing's** — *Proximal myopathy is one of the four; ask about stairs and getting up from a low chair.*
- Striae → **Best discriminates Cushing's** — *The guideline singles out striae that are reddish-purple and wider than 1 cm.*
- Facial fullness → **Common, less discriminating** — *The guideline lists facial fullness, like the dorsocervical fat pad, among features that are common in the general population. A round face alone is a weak signal.*
- Weight gain → **Common, less discriminating** — *Common and less discriminatory on its own; many features of cortisol excess are shared with ordinary obesity.*
- Acne or hirsutism → **Common, less discriminating** — *Both sit on the guideline's common, less discriminatory list; persistent hirsutism more often points to androgen excess (lesson 2.3).*
- Fatigue and poor sleep → **Common, less discriminating** — *Fatigue, insomnia and poor concentration are common and less discriminatory.*

### 05 / 19 · Rare, and usually from a prescription.  
*The evidence · stat*

Endogenous Cushing's syndrome is uncommon: European population studies put it at two to three new cases per million people a year. The commonest cause of Cushing's syndrome is iatrogenic, from glucocorticoids given as medicine. The Endocrine Society therefore asks for a drug history first, covering tablets, inhalers, injections and creams, including skin-bleaching products, and recommends testing when features are multiple and progressive.

**2–3** — new cases of endogenous Cushing's syndrome per million people per year (European population studies) (As cited in the Endocrine Society diagnostic guideline, 2008)  
*Means:* A round, puffy face in an aesthetic clinic is almost always something other than Cushing's syndrome, and when cortisol excess is real it most often comes from a glucocorticoid the patient is already using.  
*Does not mean:* It does not mean you can dismiss it. The guideline recommends testing when discriminating features are multiple and progressive or unusual for age, and some groups, such as people with poorly controlled diabetes, carry a higher rate.

### 06 / 19 · Reassure the many, refer the few.  
*What changes in practice · audio*

When a patient asks about 'cortisol face', start with the drug history: steroid creams, skin-lighteners, herbal tonics and injections. Then look for the discriminating features. Without them, the Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology guide reads most puffiness as sleep, alcohol, salt and fluid, and the Endocrine Society recommends against widespread testing. With them, especially when they are progressing, the next step is formal testing and an endocrinologist. A cortisol supplement or a consumer cortisol panel answers the wrong question.

- Drug history first, including creams and injections
- Look for bruising, plethora, proximal weakness, wide purple striae
- Refer for testing; skip supplements and consumer panels

**By role**
- *Prescriber:* If discriminating features are present, arrange one of the guideline's first-line tests or refer directly to endocrinology, and document the drug history, including creams and injections. An abnormal result goes to an endocrinologist.
- *Clinical staff:* Record any steroid creams, skin-lighteners or injections the patient mentions, note bruising or wide purple striae you see, and bring it to the prescriber before the next treatment.
- *Advisor:* Do not recommend 'cortisol-lowering' supplements or consumer cortisol kits. If a patient describes easy bruising, muscle weakness or new wide purple stretch marks, point them to a prescriber.

## Lesson 2.2 · Estrogen, menopause and dermal collagen

### 08 / 19 · Estrogen and the dermis.  
*Lesson 2.2 · Concept · audio*

Skin carries estrogen receptors (ERα, ERβ and the membrane receptor GPER) in both sun-protected and sun-damaged sites. In postmenopausal women, two weeks of topical estradiol raised collagen production in sun-protected hip skin; photoaged facial skin showed no such response. After menopause, small biopsy studies found skin collagen and thickness falling year by year, following years since menopause more closely than age. That is mechanism and natural history. What hormone therapy does to a face is a separate question.

- Receptors: ERα, ERβ and GPER in aged and photoaged skin
- Topical estradiol raises collagen in sun-protected skin only
- Collagen loss follows years since menopause

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Estrogen is the hormone the guide says dominates the visible ageing of women's skin, so start with what it does in the dermis. Skin carries estrogen receptors in sun-protected and sun-damaged sites alike. In a vehicle-controlled study, two weeks of topical estradiol raised collagen production in the hip skin of postmenopausal women; in photoaged forearm and facial skin, the same treatment made no measurable difference. Small biopsy studies from the nineteen eighties and nineties found that skin collagen and thickness fall after menopause, and that the fall follows years since the last period more closely than birthdays. Keep two questions apart as you go. One is what estrogen does in the dermis. The other is whether giving it changes how a face ages, and those answers come from different trials.

</details>

<sub>Sources: Rittié L 2008 · Thornton MJ 2013 · Brincat M 1985 · Affinito P 1999 · Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology — A Clinician's Pocket Guide 2026</sub>

### 09 / 19 · One to two per cent a year.  
*The evidence · stat*

In small biopsy studies of postmenopausal women, skin collagen content and skin thickness fell by about 1–2% a year, in step with bone mass, and followed years since menopause more than age. Reviews, and the Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology guide, quote a steeper early loss of up to 30% in the first five years. That figure traces to the same small studies, and a check of their abstracts in September 2026 did not find it stated.

**1–2%** — fall in skin collagen content and thickness per year after menopause (small biopsy studies) (Brincat et al., 1987 — in parallel with bone mass)  
*Means:* Estrogen withdrawal is a measurable contributor to dermal thinning, and it follows the menopause clock rather than age alone.  
*Does not mean:* It does not show that hormone therapy restores a younger-looking face. These are small observational studies, mostly of non-facial skin; the treatment trials come next.

<sub>Sources: Brincat M 1987 · Brincat M 1985 · Affinito P 1999 · Thornton MJ 2013 · Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology — A Clinician's Pocket Guide 2026</sub>

### 10 / 19 · Biopsy gains, mirror results.  
*Human evidence · tool*

Hormone therapy has two kinds of trial behind it. Small trials measure the dermis; larger trials measure the face. They point in different directions, so read them separately. The Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology guide grades the skin benefit B; the North American Menopause Society rates the same evidence 'limited or inconsistent'. This course grades the structural gains C and teaches the negative wrinkle trials alongside them.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Systemic HT · skin thickness and collagen | Increased · GRADE C | Two small RCTs (60 and 41 women) measured hip and upper-arm skin |
| Systemic HT · facial wrinkles | No difference · GRADE B | 485 women for 48 weeks on low-dose therapy; a four-year trial in recently menopausal women also found no difference |
| Pooled analysis (2023) | Positive, heterogeneous · GRADE C | Small studies, some non-randomized, varying widely; its authors do not propose hormone therapy as a skin treatment |
| Topical estrogen on the face | Small and mixed · GRADE C | One 24-week RCT improved thickness and fine lines; a biopsy study found no collagen response in photoaged facial skin |
| Topical estrogen · acute wounds | Faster healing · GRADE C | A small randomized study in healthy older adults |

**Callout:** A thicker dermis on biopsy and a younger-looking face are different outcomes. Ask which one a trial measured.

### 11 / 19 · Symptoms and bone. Skin is a bystander.  
*Regulatory status and indications · audio*

In the US, as of February 2026, FDA-approved menopausal hormone therapy is for moderate-to-severe hot flashes, vaginal and vulvar symptoms and, for some products, preventing bone loss. FDA's 2025–26 labeling changes removed cardiovascular, breast-cancer and dementia statements from the boxed warning; skin did not become an indication. The North American Menopause Society's 2022 statement finds benefit outweighs risk for bothersome symptoms and bone protection when therapy starts before 60 or within 10 years of menopause, and finds insufficient evidence for compounded 'bioidentical' hormones. Under US rules an over-the-counter 'hormone cream' is an unapproved drug. For the skin itself, daily sunscreen has a large randomized trial behind it, and one tretinoin cream is FDA-approved as an adjunct for fine facial wrinkles. In the UK, as of September 2026, HRT products such as estradiol patches and gel are prescription-only medicines licensed for oestrogen-deficiency symptoms and, for some, for preventing osteoporosis in women at high risk of fracture; skin is not among their uses. A compounded 'bioidentical' hormone is an unlicensed medicine in the UK, and MHRA guidance puts a licensed product first when one can meet the need. On the UK medicines compendium, topical tretinoin appears only in acne treatments.

- Indications: hot flashes, genitourinary symptoms, bone
- Low-dose vaginal estrogen for GSM: the one estrogen-for-tissue use
- For the skin itself: daily sunscreen and a retinoid

**By role**
- *Prescriber:* Prescribe hormone therapy for its labeled indications and the position statement's timing, and record that skin was discussed only as a secondary effect. Do not prescribe systemic or topical estrogen for wrinkles alone, and prefer licensed products (FDA-approved in the US, MHRA-licensed in the UK) to compounded 'bioidentical' preparations.
- *Clinical staff:* If a patient asks for 'hormones for my skin', ask about hot flashes, sleep and vaginal symptoms, record what she says, and route those symptoms to the prescriber. The skin concern stays with skin-directed care.
- *Advisor:* In the US, a cream sold as containing estrogen for the face is regulated as a drug; in the UK, HRT products are prescription-only medicines. Compounded 'bioidentical' products lack efficacy and safety data. Menopausal symptoms are a conversation for a prescriber; sun protection is advice you can give.

## Lesson 2.3 · Skin hallmarks of inherited and systemic endocrinopathies

### 13 / 19 · Some syndromes show on the skin first.  
*Lesson 2.3 · Concept · audio*

A handful of inherited endocrine syndromes appear on the skin before the glands declare themselves. In multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), multiple facial angiofibromas and collagenomas are common. In Carney complex, lentigines on the lips, eyelids and canthi, blue naevi and small skin myxomas sit alongside myxomas of the heart and cortisol-secreting adrenal disease. In MEN2A, an itchy, rippled, pigmented plaque between the shoulder blades can precede medullary thyroid cancer.

- MEN1: facial angiofibromas, collagenomas
- Carney complex: lip and eyelid lentigines, myxomas
- MEN2A: lichen amyloidosis between the shoulder blades

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Some endocrine syndromes are written on the skin years before the glands give them away. In multiple endocrine neoplasia type one, most patients in one careful series had several facial angiofibromas, the same lesions seen in tuberous sclerosis, along with collagenomas on the trunk. In Carney complex, usually caused by inactivating variants in the PRKAR1A gene, freckle-like lentigines cluster on the lips, eyelids and the corners of the eyes, alongside blue naevi and small skin myxomas. The same patients can develop myxomas in the heart and cortisol-secreting adrenal disease. In MEN two A, an itchy, rippled, darkened patch between the shoulder blades, called cutaneous lichen amyloidosis, can appear long before medullary thyroid cancer. Each of these is a referral finding, and none of them is yours to diagnose alone.

</details>

<sub>Sources: Darling TN 1997 · Kirschner LS 2000 · Correa R 2015 · Stanescu LS 2024 · Lause M 2017 · Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology — A Clinician's Pocket Guide 2026</sub>

### 14 / 19 · Eighty-eight per cent.  
*The evidence · stat*

In a consecutive series of 32 people with MEN1 examined at the US National Institutes of Health, 88% had multiple facial angiofibromas and 72% had collagenomas. The angiofibromas were clinically and histologically identical to those of tuberous sclerosis. The authors concluded that angiofibromas in someone without tuberous sclerosis call for biochemical testing for MEN1.

**88%** — of 32 people with MEN1 had multiple facial angiofibromas (Darling et al., 1997 — collagenomas in 72%)  
*Means:* Multiple facial angiofibromas are common in MEN1 and can be its first visible sign; in someone without tuberous sclerosis they are a reason for endocrine testing, whatever the cosmetic plan.  
*Does not mean:* It does not mean a single facial papule is MEN1. This is one small referral-centre series, and angiofibromas have other causes, tuberous sclerosis above all.

<sub>Sources: Darling TN 1997 · Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology — A Clinician's Pocket Guide 2026</sub>

### 15 / 19 · The everyday endocrine signs.  
*Concept · audio*

Most endocrine skin signs you meet come from common disease. New generalised darkening, strongest in skin creases, scars, mucosa and sun-exposed skin, can signal primary adrenal insufficiency, which can turn into a life-threatening crisis. Hirsutism that appears fast, keeps progressing, or comes with a deepening voice or clitoromegaly makes an androgen-secreting tumour more likely. Velvety dark plaques of acanthosis nigricans track insulin resistance, pretibial myxoedema points to Graves' disease, and cold, dry skin suggests hypothyroidism.

- Darkening in creases, scars and gums: adrenal insufficiency
- Rapid hirsutism or virilization: exclude a tumour
- Acanthosis, pretibial plaques, dry cool skin: metabolic and thyroid

## For the author

1. A&RE ch.9 — 'skin collagen falls … on the order of 30% within the first five years, then roughly 2% per year' (refs 1 and 3, both reviews). The primary biopsy studies it traces to report 1–2% a year tracking years since menopause (Brincat 1985, PMID 3978054; Brincat 1987, PMID 3120067; Affinito 1999, PMID 10656502); none of their abstracts states the 30% figure, which appears in reviews (e.g., Thornton 2013). The course teaches 1–2% a year and names 30% as a review figure. Suggest citing the primary study that reports it, or dropping it.
2. A&RE ch.9 grades the skin effect of MHT GRADE B, citing the NAMS 2022 statement. NAMS itself rates that key point Level II (limited or inconsistent) on the strength of one review. The largest RCT of HT on facial skin (Phillips 2008, n=485, PMID 18625536) and the 4-year KEEPS skin study (Owen 2016, PMID 27393520) found no difference in wrinkles; neither is cited by the guide. The course grades structural effects (thickness, collagen) C and says a visible wrinkle benefit has not been shown in the largest trials. The guide may wish to revisit B.
3. A&RE ch.9 ref 8 (Pivazyan 2023) — pools small, heterogeneous studies including non-randomized ones (I² 64–98%), omits KEEPS, and its own authors say MHT should not be considered an independent treatment for skin ageing. Suggest describing it as low-certainty support.
4. A&RE ch.8 'cortisol face' — the opening description of the 'genuine endocrine entity' leads with moon facies and dorsocervical/supraclavicular fat pads, which the Endocrine Society guideline (Nieman 2008, Table 1) lists among common, less discriminatory features; its best discriminators are easy bruising, facial plethora, proximal myopathy and reddish-purple striae >1 cm. The chapter's clinical pearl already lists the right red flags; suggest the description lead with them.
5. A&RE ch.8 — worth adding the guideline's first step: a drug history for exogenous glucocorticoids, including topical and skin-bleaching creams, herbal tonics and joint or nerve injections (Nieman 2008, 3.1), since iatrogenic Cushing's is the commonest form and the most relevant to aesthetic practice.
6. A&RE inherited-syndromes chapter — the Carney complex entry omits cardiac myxomas, present in 20–40% of patients and responsible for more than half of disease mortality (Correa 2015, PMID 26130139). They are the main reason a lentigines-plus-myxoma pattern is a referral. PRKAR1A '~70%' matches Correa 2015 ('more than 70%').
7. A&RE ch.9 — 'topical estrogen creams … are absorbed systemically' is not stated in the cited review's abstract (Shu & Maibach 2011) and was not verified; the course teaches the US regulatory position instead (21 CFR 310.530: an OTC 'hormone cream' is an unapproved new drug).
8. A&RE ch.9 and ch.14 — FDA's menopausal hormone therapy labeling action (request 10 Nov 2025; first six approved labels 12 Feb 2026: cardiovascular, breast-cancer and probable-dementia statements removed from the boxed warning) is not mentioned. Suggest adding it, date-stamped, because it changes how the risk conversation is framed; approved indications did not change and do not include skin.
9. A&RE algorithms pp.93–96 are images only; the course used them to locate claims and sourced every teaching statement to a primary or guideline record. The menopause algorithm's 'topical estrogen off-label (C)' box would read more safely as 'not recommended for wrinkles' to match ch.9's text.
10. All other A&RE ch.8, ch.9 and inherited-syndrome references resolved on PubMed with matching title, first author, journal and year (Slominski 2000, Tiganescu 2011, Schoepe 2006, Nieman 2008, Hall 2005, Thornton 2013, Brincat 2005, NAMS 2022 and 2020, Shu 2011, Manson 2013, Pivazyan 2023, Lause 2017, Darling 1997, Stanescu 2024, Kirschner 2000).
11. Not in either guide, worth adding — Ajjan 2022 (first human RCT of 11β-HSD1 inhibition in skin; pilot, primary outcome missed), Phillips 2008 and Owen 2016 (negative HT wrinkle trials), Martin 2018 (Endocrine Society hirsutism guideline: rapid progression or virilization → androgen-secreting tumour, ~0.2%, over half malignant), Bornstein 2016 (hyperpigmentation distribution in primary adrenal insufficiency), Correa 2015 (Carney complex cardiac myxomas), Hughes 2013 (sunscreen RCT, n=903) and the Renova label (the one FDA-approved adjunct for fine facial wrinkles).
12. UK status (2026-09-25) — neither guide gives a UK position for menopausal hormone therapy or tretinoin. UK HRT licences (e.g. Evorel patches, Sandrena gel) cover oestrogen-deficiency symptoms and, for some products, osteoporosis prevention; a compounded 'bioidentical' preparation is an unlicensed medicine in the UK (MHRA Guidance Note 14 places any licensed product that meets the need first). The UK medicines compendium lists topical tretinoin only in acne combinations (Treclin, Aknemycin Plus), so the guides' 'prescription retinoid for photoageing' advice reads as US practice for a UK reader. MHRA's products database could not confirm an absence for tretinoin (its full-text search returns 289 documents), so the course states only what the compendium lists.
