### 02 / 20 · GRADE rates certainty in an effect, one outcome at a time.  
*Lesson 7.1 · Concept · audio*

GRADE sorts the evidence on a specific question into four levels of certainty: high, moderate, low and very low. The rating applies to a body of evidence for one outcome, never to a single study or to a molecule. Randomized trials start high and observational studies start low; certainty then falls for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision or suspected publication bias, and occasionally rises for a very large effect. Both Hormonaly guides print the letters A to D for the same idea, adapted, and attach each letter to a specific use.

- Four levels, applied to a body of evidence
- Five named reasons to rate down
- The letter belongs to a use, never to a molecule

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Every claim you hear about a skin peptide carries a hidden question. How sure can we be? GRADE answers it with four levels of certainty, from high to very low. The rating belongs to a body of evidence for one outcome, such as wrinkle depth at twelve weeks, and never to a single study or to the molecule itself. Randomized trials start high and observational studies start low. From there, certainty drops for five reasons you can name, which are risk of bias, inconsistency between trials, indirectness, imprecision, and suspected publication bias. It can occasionally rise, for example when an effect is very large. Both Hormonaly guides print a letter from A to D beside each use. Read those letters as the same four levels, adapted. They rate the evidence for that use, and they say nothing about whether a product is legal.

</details>

### 03 / 20 · Sponsored studies come out better.  
*Who paid · stat*

A Cochrane methodology review brought together 75 papers, each comparing studies sponsored by the manufacturer with studies funded in other ways. Industry-sponsored drug and device studies more often reported favourable efficacy results and favourable conclusions, and the difference was not explained by the usual risk-of-bias checks. It is the pattern you met with oral collagen in module 04.

**1.27** — risk ratio for a favourable efficacy result, industry-sponsored versus other studies (Lundh et al., Cochrane 2017 · 95% CI 1.17–1.37 · moderate-certainty evidence)  
*Means:* When a manufacturer pays for the trial, a favourable result is more likely. Funding is a reason to read a trial harder and to look for independent replication.  
*Does not mean:* It does not make a sponsored trial wrong or an independent one right. It describes an average tilt across many studies and says nothing about any single product.

### 04 / 20 · Name what lowers certainty.  
*Worked example · tool*

GRADE names the ways a body of evidence can mislead. Read the four you will use most. Then sort four cases from this course by the reason that most lowers certainty. The fifth, publication bias, is suspected when the evidence is a handful of small, mostly commercially funded studies.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Risk of bias | How the trials were run | Flaws in design or conduct that can exaggerate an effect; check whether low- and high-quality trials disagree |
| Inconsistency | The trials disagree | Unexplained differences in results between studies of the same question |
| Indirectness | A different question answered | The studies differ from your question in population, intervention, comparison or outcome |
| Imprecision | Too little data | Too few people or events; the confidence interval spans an important effect and little or none |

**Callout:** Name the reason and you can explain a grade to a patient in one sentence.

**Exercise (quickfire) · Name the problem** — One case at a time: which reason most lowers certainty here?
- Topical GHK-Cu: the only indexed trial measured healing after laser resurfacing. Your patient asks about photoageing. → **Indirectness** — *Indirectness. The trial's outcome, healing after resurfacing, is a neighbour of your patient's question, photoageing, so it answers a different question.*
- LL-37 in venous leg ulcers: a 34-person trial was positive; a 148-person multicentre trial was negative in its full cohort. → **Inconsistency** — *Inconsistency. Two randomized trials of the same question point in different directions, and the larger one found no effect overall.*
- Oral collagen: the pooled benefit disappears when only high-quality trials are kept. → **Risk of bias** — *Risk of bias. When low- and high-quality trials disagree, GRADE gives the high-quality ones the weight, and here they show no effect.*
- BPC-157 for skin healing: the wound data come from animal models, mostly rodents. → **Indirectness** — *Indirectness. The population is animals and your question is about people, as indirect as evidence gets.*

### 05 / 20 · Two checks, never one.  
*What changes in practice · audio*

Evidence grade and regulatory status are separate axes, and both Hormonaly guides ask you to clear both. An approved drug can carry a low grade for the use a patient wants, and a compound with stronger human data can still have no lawful route. A plausible mechanism never raises a grade. Say the outcome, the grade and who ran the trials, then check the status as its own step.

- Grade: certainty for this outcome
- Status: what the law allows, checked separately
- Mechanism never raises a grade

**By role**
- *Prescriber:* Before you recommend or prescribe, write the outcome and its grade in the note, and record the product's regulatory status on a separate line. If no trial addresses the outcome the patient wants, say so and record it.
- *Clinical staff:* When a patient quotes a result, note which outcome it was and where it came from, and pass questions about grades and status to the prescriber rather than rating the product yourself.
- *Advisor:* Describe what a product has been shown to do, for which outcome, and who funded the trials. Leave the question of whether it can lawfully be prescribed to a prescriber.

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Suppose a patient asks whether a peptide works. Start with the outcome they care about, say fewer lines around the eyes, and give the certainty for that outcome, along with who paid for the trials behind it. Then, as a separate step, check what the product legally is. The Hormonaly guides keep these two checks apart for a reason. An approved drug can carry a low grade for the particular use a patient has in mind, and a compound with stronger human data may still have no lawful route in the United States. A persuasive mechanism leaves the grade exactly where it is. Whatever your role, say the outcome and the grade in one breath, and treat the regulatory answer as a question of its own.

</details>

## Lesson 7.2 · Compounding law, the 40-amino-acid line and certificates of analysis

### 07 / 20 · Two compounding routes, one strict order.  
*Lesson 7.2 · Concept · audio*

In the US, section 503A lets a licensed pharmacist or physician compound for an identified patient on a prescription, exempt from new-drug approval, adequate directions for use and good manufacturing practice. The bulk substance must meet a USP or NF monograph; failing that, be a component of an approved drug; failing that, appear on FDA's 503A bulks list. It must also come from an FDA-registered establishment with a valid certificate of analysis. Section 503B outsourcing facilities register with FDA, work to good manufacturing practice under inspection, and may compound without patient-specific prescriptions.

- 503A: patient-specific, three exemptions
- Monograph, then approved component, then the bulks list
- 503B: registered, inspected, held to good manufacturing practice

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Two routes exist for compounding in the United States, and the difference between them shapes the rest of this lesson. Under section 503A, a licensed pharmacist or physician prepares a drug for an identified patient on a prescription. If the conditions are met, the product escapes three requirements, namely approval as a new drug, labelling with adequate directions, and good manufacturing practice. The conditions start with the substance itself, taken in strict order. A USP or National Formulary monograph comes first. Only when there is none can it be a component of an approved drug, and only when neither applies can it sit on FDA's 503A bulks list. The substance must also come from a registered manufacturer with a valid certificate of analysis. Section 503B outsourcing facilities work differently. They register with FDA, work to good manufacturing practice, face risk-based inspection, and can compound without a prescription for each patient. Their products remain unapproved.

</details>

### 09 / 20 · Forty amino acids.  
*Peptide or biologic · stat*

Since 23 March 2020 FDA defines a protein as an alpha amino acid polymer of more than 40 amino acids, whether or not it is chemically synthesized, so anything above the line is a biological product. On that date tesamorelin, a 44-residue GHRH analogue, and the enzyme hyaluronidase moved to biologics licences, and FDA told compounders that transitioned biologics cannot use the 503A or 503B exemptions.

**40** — amino acids: above this, FDA treats the molecule as a protein, and so as a biological product (85 FR 10057, effective 23 March 2020 · tesamorelin (44 residues) and hyaluronidase moved to biologics licences)  
*Means:* Size decides the rulebook. Peptides of 40 or fewer alpha amino acids stay drugs, so the 503A questions apply; above 40 the product is a biologic, and the compounding exemptions are closed to it.  
*Does not mean:* It does not make a short peptide lawful to compound; BPC-157 (15 residues) and GHK (3) still need a 503A route. How residues are counted can be contested: a court upheld FDA's count of 39 for retatrutide in 2025, and the appeal was still open at the latest filings (June 2026).

### 10 / 20 · What a certificate can and cannot prove.  
*Concept · audio*

A certificate of analysis is the supplier's own report on a named lot. Mass spectrometry checks identity, and a careful certificate prints the expected mass beside the observed one. HPLC gives purity as a percentage, which says nothing about what the impurities are. Endotoxin, microbial, water, counter-ion, metal and solvent results complete it. A certificate cannot prove that the vial in your hand came from that lot, how it was stored since, or that it is sterile and suitable to inject.

- Identity: mass spectrometry, observed against expected
- Purity: an HPLC percentage, impurities unnamed
- Suitability to inject needs sterility, endotoxin and the right lot

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Picture the certificate a patient hands you, and read it line by line, asking what each line actually tests. Mass spectrometry checks identity, and a careful certificate shows the expected mass next to the one measured. HPLC reports purity as a percentage of the main peak, which tells you how much else is present and nothing about what that something else is. Endotoxin matters for anything injected, and FDA's guidance says the acceptable limit is calculated from the dose and the route, so a bare figure settles little. When FDA reviewed BPC-157 for its advisory committee, most public certificates listed purity alone, and the one a nominator supplied mentioned no endotoxin test. Then remember what paper cannot do. A certificate describes a lot. It cannot show that your vial came from that lot, how the vial was stored, or that it is sterile.

</details>

**Exercise (classify) · Useful or red flag?** — Tap what each line is worth.
- Lot number printed, matching the vial label → **Useful, if it matches the vial** — *A matching lot number links the paper to the product in hand. It is still the supplier's own claim.*
- Purity 99.2% by HPLC, no chromatogram → **Red flag** — *A bare percentage cannot be checked, and it says nothing about which impurities make up the rest.*
- Identity: expected and observed mass printed, spectrum attached → **Useful, if it matches the vial** — *That is how identity is shown. It confirms the molecule and tells you nothing about sterility.*

- Endotoxin: not tested, on a product sold for injection → **Red flag** — *Endotoxin is a critical attribute for anything injected, and its limit depends on dose and route. 'Not tested' on an injectable is a hard stop.*
- Tested 20 months before purchase → **Red flag** — *Peptides degrade in storage; the Peptide Pocket Guide treats a certificate more than 12 months older than the purchase as a warning sign.*

### 12 / 20 · 99% on the label.  
*Label against laboratory · stat*

Researchers made test purchases of semaglutide from illegal online pharmacies and analysed what arrived. The three vials that were delivered were all judged probably substandard or falsified: purity measured 7.7–14.4% against the 99% printed on the label, peptide content ran 29–39% above the labelled amount, and endotoxin was found in every vial, although none grew microorganisms.

**7.7–14.4%** — measured purity of online semaglutide vials labelled 99% (Ashraf et al., J Med Internet Res 2024 · three delivered vials · endotoxin detected in all three)  
*Means:* Purity printed on a label can be wildly wrong, and a vial that grows nothing can still carry endotoxin. Independent testing found what the paperwork hid.  
*Does not mean:* Three vials of one drug give no market-wide failure rate, and semaglutide is outside the skin-peptide shelf. It documents the gap between claim and content for one product; it does not measure research peptides as a class.

### 13 / 20 · Research use only is a label, and never a route.  
*What changes in practice · audio*

In the US, 'not for use in humans' labelling lets a supplier ship an investigational drug for laboratory or in-vitro research, and obliges the shipper to check that is how it will be used. FDA reads intended use from claims, marketing and the circumstances of sale, so the disclaimer changes nothing about a vial sold with injection instructions. A certificate cannot turn such a product into a pharmaceutical.

- The label permits laboratory research only
- Intended use is read from how a product is sold
- A certificate cannot convert a research product

**By role**
- *Prescriber:* Do not prescribe, supply, supervise or recommend a research-use-only product, and do not read its certificate as permission. Record the product, lot and supplier the patient names, and your advice to stop.
- *Clinical staff:* If a patient brings a research-use-only vial or its certificate, record the product name, lot and supplier exactly as printed, do not handle or administer it, and tell the prescriber the same day.
- *Advisor:* You can explain what a certificate does and does not show. You cannot vouch for a research product, and anyone injecting one should see a prescriber.

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Research use only sounds like a category. In law it works as a shipping permission. The US rule lets a supplier send an investigational drug for laboratory or test-tube work if the label says it must stay out of people, and the shipper is meant to confirm that is how it will be used. FDA judges what a product is for by how it is marketed and sold, so a disclaimer on a vial offered with injection instructions carries no weight. The Hormonaly guides say it plainly. Research use only is not a clinical pathway. A certificate of analysis, however clean, cannot convert that vial into a pharmaceutical, because sterility, manufacturing standards and a lawful route are all still missing. Whatever your role, treat these products as outside clinical care, and write down exactly what you were told.

</details>

## Lesson 7.3 · Cosmetic, compounded or investigational: consent and the chart

### 15 / 20 · Off-label needs a label.  
*Lesson 7.3 · Concept · audio*

Off-label prescribing means using an approved drug for a use its labelling does not cover, and FDA accepts it as clinical judgment. A never-approved peptide has no label to depart from, so prescribing it is use of an unapproved drug. Introducing an unapproved new drug into interstate commerce is a prohibited act under the FD&C Act, and FDA's 2020 warning letter to a compounding pharmacy named BPC-157 and GHK-Cu on exactly this basis.

- Off-label applies only to approved drugs
- A never-approved peptide has no label
- Compounded is still unapproved

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Off-label is the phrase to get right first. FDA uses it for an approved drug prescribed outside its labelling, which prescribers generally may do when they judge it medically appropriate. The idea assumes a label exists. A peptide that was never approved has none, so prescribing it counts as using an unapproved drug, and the Peptide Pocket Guide says exactly that. Compounding leaves that answer unchanged. A compounded product is unapproved by definition, and it is lawful only when the substance and the pharmacy meet the statute's conditions. Under federal law, putting an unapproved new drug into interstate commerce is a prohibited act, and in 2020 FDA warned a compounding pharmacy about its BPC-157 and GHK-Cu products on that basis. So write the real category in the chart, every time.

</details>

### 16 / 20 · Same molecule, different rulebooks.  
*Classification (US) · tool*

Route and claim decide the regime, whatever the molecule. Here is how each one treats a skin peptide in the US, as checked in September 2026.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmetic | Appearance claims only | No premarket approval. A claim to affect structure or function, such as 'regenerates cells', makes it a drug; 'cosmeceutical' has no legal meaning. |
| Dietary supplement | Oral, no disease claims | The maker is responsible for safety and labelling before sale; FDA acts after products reach the market. |

## Module check

**Q.** Closing Module 7: which statement keeps evidence, regulatory status and product quality apart?
- ✗ The certificate shows 99% purity, so the peptide is evidence-based and fine to compound. — *That blends product quality into evidence and legality. Purity says nothing about whether it works or whether any route is lawful.*
- ✔ Topical GHK-Cu: small-trial evidence (C); in the US, non-injectable use sits in 503A Category 1, under evaluation; its certificate can show identity and purity, never sterility or legality. — *Correct. Three separate answers, each with its own source.*
- ✗ GHK-Cu is in Category 1, so it is approved for skin and the evidence question is settled. — *Category 1 is an enforcement posture while FDA evaluates. It approves nothing and says nothing about evidence.*

## For the author

1. Peptide Pocket Guide, 'The FDA's regulatory line' table (peptide fundamentals) — the rows '41–99 alpha amino acids, chemically synthesized: still regulated as a drug' and '≥100: always a protein regardless of synthesis route' reflect FDA's 2018 proposal. The Further Consolidated Appropriations Act 2020 removed the 'chemically synthesized polypeptide' exception, and FDA's final rule (85 FR 10057, effective 23 March 2020) defines any alpha amino acid polymer of more than 40 amino acids as a protein; FDA did not finalize the 41–99 interpretation. Tesamorelin (44 residues, which the guide lists among polypeptides) was itself deemed a biologics licence on 23 March 2020.
2. Peptide Pocket Guide — 'biologics … are largely ineligible for 503A pharmacy compounding' and, in the Lilly v. FDA case study, '503A compounding of biologics operates under different authority': FDA's March 2020 notice to compounders states that biological products subject to licensure under PHS Act 351 (including deemed BLAs such as hyaluronidase and hCG) are not eligible for the 503A or 503B exemptions. Suggest stating it that plainly.
3. Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology ch. 3 ref 4 and ch. 20 ref 3 give the PCAC docket as 'FDA-2026-N-2979'; the Federal Register notice (91 FR 20465, doc 2026-07361) is Docket No. FDA-2025-N-6895, as the Peptide Pocket Guide prints.
4. Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology ch. 15 table, ch. 20 regulatory pearl and the drug-safety table place BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, ipamorelin and epitalon in '503A Category 2'. FDA's safety-risks page (content current 22 April 2026) lists them in the table of withdrawn nominations, in no category (ipamorelin remains in 503B Category 2), and 503A Category 2 holds six substances as of 14 May 2026. The Peptide Pocket Guide (August build) is current on this point.
5. Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology ch. 3 decision frame and the 'peptide decision framework' algorithm reduce 503A eligibility to 'on the current positive bulks list'. The statute's routes are hierarchical — USP/NF monograph, then component of an approved drug, then the bulks list — with Category 1 enforcement discretion (e.g., non-injectable GHK-Cu) a separate interim posture. The course teaches the statutory order.
6. Peptide Pocket Guide, 'For research-use-only peptides' (peptide fundamentals) — the steps offered 'if the product is being used outside its labelled purpose' (independent MS and HPLC, an endotoxin test before injectable use, dose-by-dose documentation) read as harm-reduction instructions for human use of products labelled 'Not for use in humans' (21 CFR 312.160), and sit awkwardly beside the guide's own 'the RUO disclaimer provides no legal protection'. The course omits them; suggest reframing as what a clinician records when a patient discloses use.
7. Peptide Pocket Guide — the two GRADE definition pages disagree: 'How strong is the evidence?' defines D as animal, in-vitro or mechanistic reasoning only and C as including conflicting results; 'Where the GRADE ratings come from' adds 'industry-driven claims' to D. Suggest one definition, mapped explicitly to GRADE's four certainty levels and its five rating-down domains.
8. Peptide Pocket Guide, jurisdictional snapshot — 'most malpractice policies exclude unapproved-drug claims' carries no citation and could not be verified; the course does not use it.
9. FDA's July 2026 BPC-157 briefing (fda.gov/media/193343) is primary evidence both guides could cite in their certificate-of-analysis sections: the nominators' certificates were for BPC-157 acetate while the nomination was for the free base, publicly available BPC-157 certificates mostly report purity alone with no impurity limits, and the nominator's certificate reported no endotoxin test. The same document cites a single small exploratory trial in ulcerative colitis reported only as a meeting abstract.
10. Not in either guide, worth adding — the Core GRADE series (BMJ 2025) as the current GRADE reference; the Cochrane review of industry sponsorship and research outcome (Lundh 2017); FDA's endotoxin Q&A guidance (Edition 2, March 2026) for the dose-and-route basis of endotoxin limits; FDA's 'Is It a Cosmetic, a Drug, or Both?' page, whose drug-claim examples include 'regenerate cells'; and Popławska 2019 (an undocumented GHRP-2 analogue found in a seized vial by high-resolution MS).
