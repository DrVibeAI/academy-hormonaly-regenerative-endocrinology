m1-x03 · Spot the unsupported claim · Lesson 1.2, after m1-p11

A serum page, line by line.

A fictional product page. Find the lines this lesson's evidence does not support.

  • The Hormone Cell Atlas (Science, 2026) read the RNA for 379 hormone and receptor genes in about 14 million cells and nuclei, skin among them.
    Accurate. The atlas read RNA in about 14 million cells and nuclei from 47 human tissues, skin among them; it did not measure hormone release.
  • ⚑ It proves your skin responds to the hormone in this serum.
    The atlas predicts from RNA, and its own portal calls the scores 'putative and hypothesis-generating'. It does not show that a hormone acts in skin.
  • Treatments applied to the skin act through receptors the skin already carries.
    Supported. Receptors explain how a topical could act; whether this one does is a question for a human trial.
  • ⚑ So those receptors guarantee visible results.
    A receptor gene on the map is not evidence that a product aimed at it works. The receptor is where an efficacy question starts, and the answer still needs a trial.
  • ⚑ Your skin's response always matches the strength on the label.
    The skin's own enzymes change how strongly a hormone acts once it arrives, so the response in skin can differ from what the dose predicts.
  • ⚑ Skip sunscreen on serum days, so your skin can make its own vitamin D.
    Sunscreen blocks vitamin D synthesis under artificial UV, yet field trials of daily use did not lower blood levels, and high-protection products have not been trialled. Keep recommending sun protection, and treat vitamin D as its own question.

Hint: Hold each line against what an RNA map can show, what a receptor can promise, how skin changes a hormone's strength, and what the sunscreen trials found.

Four lines overreach: an RNA prediction presented as proof, a receptor as a result, the label's strength as the skin's response, and vitamin D as a reason to drop sunscreen.
