### 03 / 19 · A plasma tripeptide with a copper habit.  
*Mechanism · audio*

GHK is a tripeptide found in human plasma, first described in 1973. Bound to copper it becomes GHK-Cu, which is proposed to deliver copper to enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and lysyl oxidase and to switch on wound-healing and antioxidant gene programmes. Most of that mechanistic literature comes from the group that discovered the peptide.

- Endogenous: GHK circulates in human plasma
- Proposed: copper delivery to matrix and antioxidant enzymes
- Mechanism register only — written largely by the originator

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Start with the molecule. GHK is a three-amino-acid peptide that circulates in human plasma; it was first described in 1973. It binds copper readily, and the copper complex, GHK-Cu, is what skin-care labels mean when they say copper peptide. The proposed mechanism is that GHK-Cu delivers copper to enzymes that build and protect the dermal matrix, such as lysyl oxidase and superoxide dismutase, and shifts gene expression toward wound healing. Two things to hold on to. First, this is a mechanism, which explains why the peptide might work and leaves open whether it does. Second, most of this literature was written by the group that discovered the peptide. Keep both in mind when you reach the trials.

</details>

### 04 / 19 · One indexed randomized trial.  
*Human evidence · stat*

A search of PubMed in September 2026 found one randomized controlled trial of topical GHK-Cu in human skin: 2006, healing after CO₂ laser resurfacing. The cosmetic trials cited for anti-ageing claims are manufacturer-sponsored and not indexed. Both Hormonaly guides grade topical use C.

**1** — indexed randomized trial of topical GHK-Cu in human skin (Miller et al., 2006 — healing after CO₂ laser resurfacing, not photoageing)  
*Means:* The human evidence for topical GHK-Cu is small: one small indexed trial plus sponsor-run cosmetic studies. GRADE C is the honest ceiling.  
*Does not mean:* It does not show that GHK-Cu fails, that it treats photoageing, or anything about injecting it. A thin evidence base is an unknown, not a negative.

### 06 / 19 · Serum yes, syringe no.  
*What changes in practice · audio*

Topical GHK-Cu is a reasonable cosmetic conversation at GRADE C. Injected GHK-Cu is a different product: no randomized trials, injection-site pain and rash are common because copper irritates tissue, and in the US the injectable route sits outside the Category 1 listing. In the UK, anything injected falls outside cosmetics law, and MHRA decides whether it is a medicine from its claims, form and use.

- Topical: modest, small-trial support
- Injectable: anecdote, common local reactions
- Say which route you mean every time

**By role**
- *Prescriber:* In the US, before prescribing a compounded topical, confirm the substance's current 503A status and that the pharmacy is working within it; in the UK, a GHK-Cu serum presented for appearance is a cosmetic, so there is nothing to prescribe. Do not prescribe GHK-Cu for injection. Record that you discussed a product with small-trial evidence and the patient's reasons for choosing it.
- *Clinical staff:* If a patient tells you they inject GHK-Cu, record the product, where it was bought and any site reactions, and bring it to the prescriber. Do not advise on amounts or technique.
- *Advisor:* Describe topical GHK-Cu products as cosmetics with modest, small-trial support. Anyone injecting a copper peptide should be pointed to a prescriber, not to a better supplier.

Here is what that means in the room. Topical GHK-Cu is a reasonable cosmetic conversation, as long as you describe the evidence honestly: small trials, modest effects, grade C. Injected GHK-Cu is not the same product in a stronger form. There are no randomized trials of injection, injection-site pain and rash are common because copper irritates tissue, and in the United States the injectable route sits outside the Category 1 listing that covers non-injectable use. In the United Kingdom, anything injected falls outside cosmetics law, and MHRA decides whether such a product is a medicine. Whatever your role, name the route every time you talk about this peptide.

## Lesson 4.2 · Signal peptides, oral collagen and SNAP-25 mimics

### 08 / 19 · Three families, one question: who ran the trial?  
*Lesson 4.2 · Concept · audio*

Signal peptides such as palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 (pal-KTTKS, Matrixyl) are meant to tell fibroblasts to make matrix. SNAP-25 mimics (Argireline and its longer cousin SNAP-8) and Leuphasyl, an enkephalin analogue, are meant to quieten expression lines. Oral collagen peptides are swallowed, not applied. All three families have a trial record, and in all three the question that matters most is who funded it.

- Signal peptides: 'make more matrix'
- SNAP-25 mimics: 'relax the line'
- Oral collagen: the supplement aisle

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Lesson two covers three families that fill the cosmetic and supplement shelves. Signal peptides, like palmitoyl pentapeptide-4, sold as Matrixyl, are fragments of matrix proteins meant to tell fibroblasts to make more collagen. SNAP-25 mimics, Argireline and its longer cousin SNAP-8, copy part of a nerve-terminal protein that botulinum toxin targets; the idea is a gentle, topical version of that effect. And oral collagen peptides are swallowed, not applied. Every one of these has trials behind it. As you go through them, keep asking one question before any other: who ran the trial, and who paid for it.

</details>

### 10 / 19 · Twenty-three trials, split by who paid.  
*The evidence · stat*

Pooled, oral collagen trials show better hydration, elasticity and wrinkle scores, and the Aesthetic & Regenerative Endocrinology guide grades them C. A 2025 meta-analysis split the same kind of trials by funding and by quality. The benefit appeared only in pharmaceutical-funded and low-quality trials. Unfunded and high-quality trials showed no effect.

**23 RCTs** — in the 2025 meta-analysis that split oral-collagen trials by funding and quality (1,474 participants)  
*Means:* The pooled benefit of oral collagen disappears when you look only at independent or high-quality trials. The course keeps the guide's GRADE C and shows why a downgrade has been proposed to its author.  
*Does not mean:* It does not show collagen is harmful, and it does not rule out benefit for an individual. It is a statement about the evidence, and it says nothing about topical peptides.

### 11 / 19 · Cosmetic, supplement, drug: three rulebooks.  
*What changes in practice · audio*

In the US, cosmetic peptides are cosmetic ingredients, not drugs, and their safety is assessed under a cosmetics framework. Oral collagen is a dietary supplement: it may not claim to treat disease, and product quality and content vary. Neither route has to prove efficacy before sale — which is why the funding question matters. In the UK, a peptide serum is a cosmetic: its responsible person needs a safety assessment and must notify the regulator before sale, and must be able to prove its claims. Oral collagen is a food supplement: no food may claim to prevent, treat or cure disease, and only authorised health claims may be used. In May 2026 the UK advertising regulator ruled a peptide serum's 'clinically proven' lifting claim misleading because the study behind it was small and uncontrolled.

- Cosmetic ingredient: no efficacy approval needed
- Dietary supplement: no disease claims; quality varies
- Say 'modest, small-trial' — never 'topical Botox'

**By role**
- *Prescriber:* Nothing here needs a prescription. If you recommend a product, recommend it on evidence, name the grade, and disclose any relationship with the brand. The 'topical Botox' claim is not one to repeat.
- *Clinical staff:* When a patient asks which serum 'really works', the accurate answer is modest effects from mostly sponsor-funded trials. Do not describe any of them as an alternative to botulinum toxin.
- *Advisor:* The claim you can stand behind is modest, small-trial evidence. Supplements cannot be sold as treating a disease; if a patient expects collagen to fix a skin condition, that is a conversation for a prescriber.

## Lesson 4.3 · Repair peptides and the compounding line

### 13 / 19 · Rodents, a fragment, and a tripeptide.  
*Lesson 4.3 · Concept · audio*

BPC-157 rests on a large rodent literature, mostly from one research group, and on uncontrolled human case series. TB-500 is not thymosin β4: it is a seven-residue fragment of it, identified by analysing the product itself, and no trial has tested the fragment. KPV, a tripeptide from the end of α-MSH, calms inflammation in mouse colitis and has no randomized human trial.

- BPC-157: rodents and case series
- TB-500: a fragment, never trialled
- KPV: mouse colitis only

<details><summary>Narration script</summary>

Lesson three moves from the cosmetic shelf to the peptides patients buy online and inject. BPC-157 is a fifteen-residue peptide first described in 1993. Its evidence is a large rodent literature, most of it from one research group, and uncontrolled human case series. TB-500 is sold as thymosin, but when anti-doping chemists analysed the product they found a seven-residue fragment, residues seventeen to twenty-three of thymosin beta-four. That fragment has never been tested in a trial. KPV is the last three amino acids of alpha-MSH; it calms inflammation in mouse colitis models, and there is no randomized human trial of it for anything. All three have plausible mechanisms, and none has a controlled human trial.

</details>

### 14 / 19 · One abstract, no published trial.  
*The evidence · stat*

As of September 2026 no randomized efficacy trial of BPC-157 in people has been published in full. FDA's July 2026 briefing found one randomized, placebo-controlled trial reported only as a 2005 meeting abstract: 53 people with ulcerative colitis, two weeks of treatment, and no significant difference from placebo. ClinicalTrials.gov adds a 2015 phase 1 safety study that never posted results, a completed 2025 single-arm product study with no results posted, and a randomized phase 1 trial after rotator-cuff surgery due to start in 2027.

**0** — randomized efficacy trials of BPC-157 in people published in full (September 2026) (One 2005 ulcerative-colitis trial exists only as a meeting abstract (no significant difference from placebo) · registered: phase 1 safety study (2015, no results) · single-arm product study (2025, no results) · randomized phase 1 after rotator-cuff repair (starts 2027))  
*Means:* Whether BPC-157 helps people heal is unknown. The only randomized human result is an abstract that showed no significant benefit; the enthusiasm rests on rodents and uncontrolled case series.  
*Does not mean:* It does not show BPC-157 is ineffective or unsafe. Absence of trials is absence of evidence, and this figure will change when the registered trials report.

### 15 / 19 · Two trials, one peptide: LL-37.  
*Worked example · tool*

LL-37, the skin's own cathelicidin, is the one repair peptide with two randomized trials in hard-to-heal venous leg ulcers. Read them in order.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| First trial (2014) | Positive at low dose | First-in-human, randomized, dose-ranging; 34 patients |

| Who ran them | The developer, both times | Sponsorship does not make a trial wrong; it is one more reason to wait for replication |
| Grade | C — inconsistent | A small positive trial followed by an adequately powered negative one |

**Callout:** A small positive trial is a hypothesis. LL-37 is what happens when someone tests it.

The course now says 'no randomized controlled trial' instead of 'no independent human trial'; the grade stays D. Worth adding to the Pocket Guide's Leuphasyl entry.
13. UK status (2026-09-25) — neither guide covers UK cosmetics or food law. For a UK reader: peptide serums are cosmetics under the assimilated Cosmetic Products Regulation 1223/2009 (Responsible Person, safety assessment, OPSS notification, claims that must be substantiated; anything injected is not a cosmetic, art 2(2)); oral collagen is a food supplement (Food Supplements (England) Regulations 2003; no disease claims, FIC art 7(3); only authorised health claims, Regulation 1924/2006 art 10). The ASA's 13 May 2026 ruling against a peptide serum's 'clinically proven' 20% lift claim (30 participants, no control) illustrates the guides' own sponsorship point.
14. Oral collagen, UK health claims (2026-09-25) — the Great Britain nutrition and health claims register (DHSC, 19 May 2026) lists collagen skin claims as non-authorised (e.g. 'helps the skin to preserve its firmness and elasticity', and the proprietary VeriSol P claim on skin elasticity and wrinkles), while 'vitamin C contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin' is authorised — which is why many collagen products add vitamin C. Worth adding to the guides' oral-collagen entry. The course does not state it yet: the register is published only as a spreadsheet, which the automated source check cannot read.
