How to use this guide
What each entry contains, how the book is ordered, and what the shaded
box on every page is for.

This pocket guide covers seventy-two entries — peptide therapeutics,
peptide-derived biologics, peptide mixtures, and peptide-adjacent
comparators (notably small-molecule oral GLP-1 receptor agonists). FDA-
and EMA-approved drugs sit alongside the investigational and
compounding-accessible agents commonly encountered in modern practice.
Each entry occupies a single page and follows the same template: a
pharmacologic class, the canonical name and any brand names, history
where a documented development record exists, sequence, mechanism,

How strong is the evidence?
The GRADE letter on every entry, what it rates, and why it is not the
same scale as the companion volume's.

Every entry carries a GRADE letter. GRADE reflects the strength of the
clinical evidence for the listed use — not mechanistic plausibility, popularity,
compounding availability, or clinical fashion.
GRADE A                      High-quality evidence. Multiple adequately powered
                             randomized trials, or one large trial with consistent
                             supporting data.
GRADE B                      Moderate. Randomized evidence with limitations, or
                             consistent observational data in humans.
GRADE C                      Low. Small or uncontrolled human studies, surrogate
                             endpoints, or conflicting results.
GRADE D                      Very low or absent human evidence. Animal, in vitro,
                             or mechanistic reasoning only.
A low GRADE is not a verdict that a molecule does not work. It states that the
published evidence cannot yet support a confident claim that it does.

A SEPARATE SCALE FROM THE COMPANION VOLUME
The Expert Witness Pocket Guide rates how settled the law is on a question,
using the letters A through D for a different purpose entirely. A molecule can
rest on settled law and weak evidence, or on strong evidence and unsettled
law. Do not read a GRADE letter here as an authority rating there.

WHY RESEARCH-ONLY MOLECULES ARE INCLUDED
This guide covers approved drugs, investigational peptides in active trials,
compounded agents, and research-use-only substances encountered in
practice. Including a gray-market peptide does not endorse its use. Patients
arrive already taking BPC-157, TB-500, and Vilon; understanding the
preclinical evidence, the known harms, and the evidentiary gaps is part of
treating them.

                             substantial observational data; conclusions reasonably

Editorial standards and evidence
philosophy
What inclusion means, what it does not mean, and how this guide treats

This guide is a clinical reference, not a recommendation or endorsement.

EVIDENCE-FIRST, BY GRADE
Every entry is rated A-D by GRADE methodology, which reflects the strength
of clinical evidence -- not molecular plausibility, popularity, compounding
availability, or fashion. An FDA-approved drug may carry a lower grade for a
particular off-label use than an investigational compound supported by
stronger human evidence; regulatory status and evidentiary certainty are
separate axes. Mechanistic appeal alone never raises a grade.

    GRADE A does not mean "use this"; GRADE D does not mean "do not."
    Clinical decisions require GRADE plus regulatory status,
    contraindications, patient preference, and judgment.


WHY INVESTIGATIONAL AND GRAY-MARKET COMPOUNDS ARE
INCLUDED

503A-compounded agents, and research-only substances seen in
gray-market settings. Inclusion is not endorsement -- it reflects clinical
reality: agents like BPC-157, TB-500, and Dihexa are used off-label without
approval, and clinicians need their preclinical evidence, known harms, and

Peptide fundamentals
Definitions, classification, manufacturing, and the regulatory grades a
single molecule can occupy.

Definitions, classification, manufacturing, regulatory grades, and how to read
a Certificate of Analysis — the foundation behind every entry in this guide.

WHAT IS A PEPTIDE?
A peptide is a short chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. The
biochemical distinction from a protein is conventionally one of length:
peptides are typically 2-50 residues; proteins are longer, fold into stable
tertiary structures, and usually exceed 10 kDa.
The boundary is genuinely fuzzy — insulin (51 residues, two disulfide-linked
chains) crosses the threshold by one residue and is variably classified as a
polypeptide or a small protein. We list it as a 51-residue exception under the
polypeptide class.

THE FDA'S REGULATORY LINE
The biochemical definition above is descriptive; the FDA's definition is
operational and consequential. Under the BPCIA (2009) as amended by the
Further Consolidated Appropriations Act (2020), the agency draws the
peptide/protein line at 40 alpha amino acids:
≤ 40 alpha amino acids        Peptide, regulated as a drug under the FD&C Act (NDA
                              pathway)
41-99 alpha amino acids,      "Chemically synthesized polypeptide" — still regulated
chemically synthesized        as a drug under the FD&C Act
> 40 alpha amino acids, not   Protein, regulated as a biological product under the
chemically synthesized        PHS Act (BLA pathway)
(recombinant or extracted)
≥ 100 alpha amino acids       Always a protein/biologic regardless of synthesis
                              route
The classification matters: biologics face additional statutory protections
(12-year exclusivity, biosimilar pathway), more rigorous CMC review, and are
largely ineligible for 503A pharmacy compounding. Drugs follow the
smaller-molecule NDA pathway with shorter exclusivity and a more
permissive compounding regime.

CLASSIFICATION BY CHAIN LENGTH
Dipeptides & tripeptides      2–3 residues. Examples: GHK (3), KPV (3)
Oligopeptides                 4–20 residues. Examples: Oxytocin (9), GnRH (10),
                              Octreotide (8), Kisspeptin-10 (10), BPC-157 (15)
Polypeptides                  20–50 residues. Examples: GLP-1 analogues (30–31),
                              Glucagon (29), Calcitonin (32), Tesamorelin (44)
Small proteins                ≥51 residues. Examples: Insulin (51, boundary case),
                              GH (191), Follistatin (315), Cerebrolysin (mixture)

WHY CHAIN LENGTH MATTERS
Residue count drives pharmacology and, near 40 residues, regulatory status:
≤ 10 residues                 Solid-phase synthesis. Half-life often minutes
                              (oxytocin ≈ 3 min); rapidly cleared by peptidases,
                              needing frequent or sustained-release dosing.
10–30 residues                Solid-phase synthesis still cost-effective. Half-life
                              extended by D-amino-acid substitution (octreotide),
                              N-methylation, cyclization, or PEGylation.
> 30 residues                 Recombinant production becomes attractive. Half-life
                              extended by fatty-acid acylation (semaglutide t½ ~7
                              days), Fc-fusion (dulaglutide), or depot (lanreotide).

A NOTE ON BIOREGULATORS
One functional class cuts across chain length: the bioregulators (Khavinson
"cytomedines") — very short di-/tripeptides claimed to regulate
tissue-specific genes. Examples here include Epitalon and Pinealon (with
Cortagen and Vesugen). Defined by mechanism rather than structure, the
category rests on one Russian group without independent Western
replication.

    The bioregulator category rests on one Russian research group without
    independent Western replication — as the GRADE C/D ratings reflect.

RECONSTITUTION
Most injectable peptides ship as lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that must
be reconstituted before use. The mechanics matter: forceful reconstitution
shears and denatures peptide, and repeated freeze-thaw and agitation are
recognised degradation routes. Published loss figures are
formulation-specific, so no single percentage applies across peptides.

Peptides are intrinsically unstable molecules. Knowing the degradation
pathways tells you how to store, when to discard, and what to look for in the
vial.

THE FIVE DEGRADATION PATHWAYS
Oxidation                      Of methionine, cysteine, and tryptophan residues.
                               Mitigated by antioxidants and oxygen-free packaging.
Hydrolysis                     Of peptide bonds in solution — accelerated by
                               extreme pH, heat, and time. Lyophilized form is
                               dramatically more stable.
Deamidation                    Of asparagine and glutamine to acidic forms — a
                               major cause of slow potency loss in stored solutions,
                               occurring even refrigerated.
Aggregation                    Peptides dimerize, oligomerize, or precipitate at high
                               concentration. Surfactants and gentle handling help.
Adsorption                     Peptides stick to glass and plastic surfaces, silently
                               depleting low-concentration solutions over weeks.

VISUAL SIGNS OF DEGRADED PRODUCT
— Cloudy or particulate solution after reconstitution
— Yellowing of the powder or solution
— Loss of clinical effect at a previously effective dose
— Precipitate that fails to redissolve on gentle warming
— Off-smell from the vial (rare but a hard signal)

SOLID-PHASE PEPTIDE SYNTHESIS (SPPS)
Invented by Bruce Merrifield in 1963 (Nobel Prize 1984). The peptide is built
one amino acid at a time on a polymer resin.
Each cycle deprotects the chain, couples the next Fmoc-protected amino
acid, and washes — typically 30-60 minutes per residue.
After assembly, the peptide is cleaved from resin, side-chains deprotected,
and the crude product purified by reverse-phase HPLC.
Yields fall geometrically: 98% per coupling step x 50 cycles ≈ 36% overall
yield.
The practical ceiling for routine pharmaceutical manufacture is ~50 residues.
Beyond that, recombinant approaches become more economical.
— Resin bead + AA1
— + AA2
— + AA3 ...
— Cleave
— Crude peptide
— HPLC >= 98% pure

mammalian CHO cells (Fc-fusion drugs).
Used for insulin, growth hormone, and most GLP-1 analogues; the

The same molecule can exist in five radically different regulatory states.
Understanding the hierarchy is essential before prescribing or
recommending any peptide.

FDA
FDA-approved (or equivalent). An effective approved application, with a
labeled indication, dosing, contraindications, and safety information.
Approval may be traditional or accelerated: an accelerated approval can rest
on an intermediate or surrogate endpoint in a small trial, so approval alone
does not imply a completed Phase 3 program. Manufactured to cGMP with
post-market surveillance. Coverage and reimbursement are separate
questions from approval.
— Examples: insulin, semaglutide, tesamorelin, leuprolide, octreotide.
— About half the peptides in this guide fall into this category.

503B
503B outsourcing facility. Compounded under cGMP for office-use stock.
Registered with the FDA and inspected. The compound itself is not
FDA-approved — only the manufacturing standard is. Used for shortages,
off-label needs, or patient-specific requirements. Quality bar is high;
documentation is comprehensive.

503A
503A compounding pharmacy. State-licensed pharmacy compounding
against a specific patient prescription; a different regulatory bar from 503B
rather than simply a lower one. The bulk substance must satisfy a statutory
pathway: the 503A Bulks List at 21 C.F.R. 216.23, an applicable USP or NF
monograph, or a component of an FDA-approved drug.
In September 2023 FDA's 503A Category 2 list identified several peptides —
BPC-157, TB-500, melanotan II among them — as raising significant safety
risks for compounding. That roster has since changed: as of 14 May 2026
Category 2 holds six substances, the peptide-class entries being ibutamoren
mesylate and kisspeptin-10, and most of the 2023 peptides appear on FDA's
table of withdrawn nominations rather than in any category. Category status
is an interim enforcement posture, not the statutory Bulks List at 21 C.F.R.
216.23.

RUO
Research Use Only. Sold by chemical-supply companies (Bachem, Sigma,
Anaspec, others) labeled "for research only — not for human consumption."
Purity varies from pharmaceutical-grade (Bachem) to unverified (smaller
suppliers). Outside pharmaceutical regulation.

GRAY
Gray market / underground. "Peptide research" websites with deliberate
ambiguity. Quality ranges from acceptable to dangerous. May contain wrong
peptides, sub-potent material, bacterial endotoxin, residual TFA, or heavy
metals. No regulatory oversight.
— Purchased material has failed independent analysis: three semaglutide vials
  bought from illegal online sellers contained 28.6-38.7% more peptide than
  labelled, at 7.7-14.4% purity against a 99% claim (Ashraf et al. J Med
  Internet Res 2024;26:e65440 (PMID 39509151)).

OVERSIGHT HIERARCHY
— FDA approved
— 503B outsourcing facility (cGMP)
— 503A compounding pharmacy
— Research Use Only (RUO)
— Gray market / underground

  The RUO disclaimer provides no legal protection — the FDA regulates
  based on inferred intended use, not the label on the product.


A COA is the supplier's own quality attestation, not an independent
certification. A serious COA tells you what the supplier found on a named lot:
whether the mass matches the expected molecule, how pure the material
was by HPLC, what counter-ion it carries, and what the endotoxin and
microbial results were. What it cannot establish on its own is that a product
is safe to inject — that turns on sterility, the dose and route, the conditions
the material was held in since testing, and whether the lot tested is the lot in
hand.

IDENTITY
Mass spectrometry confirms the molecular weight matches the expected
peptide. The theoretical monoisotopic mass should be reported alongside the
observed mass. Deviations > 1 Da indicate sequence problems.

WATER CONTENT
Karl Fischer titration. Lyophilized peptides typically retain 3–10% residual
moisture. Higher water content reduces stability.

COUNTER-ION (TFA / ACETATE)
Synthetic peptides ship as TFA or acetate salts. A 5 mg "peptide" vial may
contain 4.2 mg net peptide after subtracting the counter-ion. Pharmaceutical
preparations always report this.

ENDOTOXIN (LAL)
Endotoxin is measured by the bacterial endotoxins test (Limulus amebocyte
lysate). There is no single universal per-milligram limit: the allowable
endotoxin for a parenteral product is derived from the maximum human dose
and the route, so the same concentration can pass for one product and fail
for another. A COA reporting endotoxin without stating the dose and route it
was judged against has not established suitability for injection.

  RUO peptides frequently have no endotoxin testing — a hard
  contraindication to injection.


MICROBIAL CONTAMINATION
Total aerobic count, yeast and mold count, absence of pathogens. Critical for
any injectable preparation.

HEAVY METALS
Lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic by ICP-MS. Within ICH Q3D limits.

RESIDUAL SOLVENTS
DMF, DMSO, methanol from SPPS. Within ICH Q3C limits.

WHAT YOU SHOULD NOT SEE ON A SERIOUS COA
— A bare claim of "purity > 99%" with no chromatogram or supporting data.
— No mass spectrum, or a spectrum without a labelled expected mass.
— No batch or lot number; no manufacture date.
— A COA dated more than 12 months before purchase — peptides degrade in
  storage.
— Generic identical-looking COAs across different products from the same
  vendor.
— No endotoxin or microbial data on a product marketed for injection.
— A COA referring to a "typical" batch rather than the specific batch shipped.

PRACTICAL HEURISTIC
A lot-specific COA supplied with the vial, naming the batch shipped, is the
minimum documentation to look for. Responsiveness to a documentation
request is a commercial signal rather than a quality standard, but an
unanswered request leaves the material undocumented.

  If you must request a COA and the response is delayed, generic, or never
  arrives, the product is probably not what it claims to be — and certainly
  not safe to inject.

FOR 503A/503B COMPOUNDED PEPTIDES
Compounding pharmacies should provide a COA documenting identity,
purity, sterility, and endotoxin. Ask which testing laboratory ran the analysis,
the date of testing, the USP-797 / 800 compliance status of the facility, and
the lot number that links the COA to the vial in hand.
A pharmacy that cannot produce this documentation on request is not
operating to the standards their license requires.

FOR RESEARCH-USE-ONLY PEPTIDES
If the product is being used outside its labelled purpose, the standard of
evidence shifts to the user. At minimum:
— Confirm identity by independent mass spectrometry.
— Confirm purity by independent HPLC.
— Run an endotoxin test before any injectable use.

Clinical pearls

A THREE-TIER PRESCRIBING FRAMEWORK
Tier 1 · approved, on-label    Highest evidence; the default when an approved agent
                               fits the need. Semaglutide for type 2 diabetes,
                               tirzepatide for obesity, tesamorelin for
                               HIV-associated lipodystrophy.
Tier 2 · approved, off-label   Lawful. Document the rationale and obtain informed
                               consent. Tesamorelin for non-HIV visceral adiposity;
                               sermorelin in adults outside approved pediatric use.
Tier 3 · not approved          Compounded or research-only. Elevated risk. This is
                               not off-label prescribing: there is no approved label to
                               depart from. BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, ipamorelin,
                               GHK-Cu, Vilon.

FOUNDATIONS BEFORE PEPTIDES
— Optimize the substrate first. Peptides amplify a healthy foundation — diet,
  sleep, hydration, training — they do not substitute for one.
— Start low, go slow. Begin at the lowest plausible dose and titrate on
  response.
— One variable at a time. Stacking several agents at once makes an adverse
  effect impossible to attribute.
— Set a stopping rule before starting, and a date to reassess.

THE TIER 3 CONVERSATION
1.    Name the article and its status plainly: not approved for this use, or not
      approved at all.
2. State what is known and what is not, including that the evidence may be
   animal or in vitro only.
3. Name the approved alternatives, including doing nothing.
4. Record the monitoring plan and the threshold to stop.

PEARL 5: CYCLING PATTERNS REPORTED IN WELLNESS PRACTICE
Commonly reported: 8-12 weeks on, 4-8 weeks off. No comparative trial

A cross-class summary of the signals reported across the incretin agents.

WHY A REWARD-PATHWAY SIGNAL IS PLAUSIBLE
GLP-1 receptors are expressed in mesolimbic reward circuitry — the ventral
tegmental area and nucleus accumbens — where activation modulates
dopaminergic signaling. This gives a mechanistic rationale for the recurring
observation that GLP-1 receptor agonists may reduce alcohol and nicotine
consumption and blunt some reward-driven behaviors. Plausibility is not
efficacy: the human evidence remains early.

ALCOHOL USE DISORDER (AUD)
— Semaglutide: the strongest prospective signal, now in dedicated trials (e.g.,
  SEMALCO) after observational and early clinical data suggested reduced
  intake.

  observational datasets, but heavily confounded.
— Dulaglutide: linked to reduced alcohol use during a smoking-cessation
  study (Probst et al., JCI Insight 2023).
— Liraglutide and exenatide: show preclinical/observational signals.

SMOKING CESSATION
The clearest randomized signal is for exenatide: one positive RCT in
combination with a nicotine patch. Other agents have observational or
preclinical data only.

INTERPRETATION FOR PRESCRIBERS
None of these is an approved indication, and none should drive drug
selection today. The class-wide consistency is worth knowing — especially
when a patient with obesity or T2DM also has AUD or tobacco dependence —
but dedicated Phase 3 trials are still in progress.

  Treat any addiction benefit as a possible secondary observation, not a
  reason to prescribe.

Receptor desensitization and
peptide cycling
Why many peptides lose effect with continuous use, and what follows for

Why many peptides lose effect with continuous use, and the rationale offered
for scheduled off-cycles ("peptide holidays"). The mechanism is well
characterised; the specific schedules are practice convention.
Many peptide effects fade with sustained or frequent dosing (tachyphylaxis),
usually from adaptive down-regulation of the target receptor — sometimes
compounded by counter-regulatory feedback or anti-drug antibodies.
Matching dosing to the body's natural rhythm, and the rationale offered for
scheduled off-cycles is that receptor responsiveness may recover during the
interval.

HOW DESENSITIZATION HAPPENS
Receptor uncoupling &      Agonist binding recruits GPCR kinases and β-arrestin,
internalization            which uncouple the receptor from its G-protein and
                           draw it into the cell (endocytosis); sustained signaling
                           then lowers receptor number (down-regulation).
Exposure pattern           Pulsatile, physiologic signaling preserves sensitivity;
                           continuous or supraphysiologic exposure (long-acting
                           analogues, frequent high doses) desensitizes fastest —
                           hence pulsatile GH secretagogues that mimic the
                           overnight rhythm hold up better.
Counter-regulatory         The body opposes sustained stimulation — e.g.,
feedback                   somatostatin tone rises against continuous GH-axis
                           drive — blunting response independent of the
                           receptor.
Anti-drug antibodies       Repeated exposure to a foreign peptide can raise
                           neutralizing antibodies that cut efficacy or, rarely,
                           cause hypersensitivity — likelier with impure
                           gray-market product.

The regulatory landscape
What makes a peptide a drug, the two compounding pathways, and why
the label on the vial does not settle the question.

WHAT MAKES A PEPTIDE A DRUG
Regulatory status follows the article and its intended use, not the syringe.
Under the FDCA a drug is any article intended to treat disease or to affect the
structure or any function of the body, and no new drug may enter interstate
commerce without an effective approved application. Intended use is read
objectively — from labeling claims, advertising, statements, and the
circumstances of distribution — so status does not change because a label
disclaims the contemplated use.
Route enters later, and it matters. FDA's safety concerns about compounded
peptides are route-specific: immunogenicity from aggregation, and difficulty
characterizing peptide-related impurities. Route appears inside individual
FDA entries for that reason — GHK-Cu is treated separately for injectable and
non-injectable administration; GHRP-2 was flagged for injectable and nasal
routes. It also determines whether sterility and cGMP obligations attach.
What it does not do is convert a never-approved peptide into a lawful article:
a topical preparation is an unapproved new drug as much as an injection is.

  Off-label prescribing presupposes an approved product to be off the
  label of; for a never-approved peptide the marketing prohibition is
  unrelieved and the adequate-directions exemption is unavailable.

THE TWO COMPOUNDING PATHWAYS
503A (21 U.S.C. 353a)        Compounding by a licensed pharmacist in a
                             state-licensed pharmacy or federal facility, or by a
                             licensed physician, on a prescription for an individual
                             patient. Compliance exempts the product from three
                             sections only: cGMP, adequate directions for use, and
                             new drug approval.
503B (21 U.S.C. 353b)        Compounding in a registered outsourcing facility,
                             which need not be a licensed pharmacy and may
                             compound without patient-specific prescriptions —
                             what permits office stock. Its exemption omits cGMP.
Bulk drug substance          By regulation, the same as an active pharmaceutical
                             ingredient.
Certificate of analysis      A statutory condition under 503A, alongside
                             manufacture by an FDA-registered establishment. The
                             statute sets no purity figure; 98% by HPLC is a
                             purchasing norm, while compendial and cGMP
                             standards fix purity, sterility and endotoxin limits.
Under 503A the three routes to bulk eligibility are hierarchical, not
alternative. A substance must comply with an applicable USP or NF
monograph where one exists. Only if no monograph exists may it qualify as a
component of an FDA-approved drug. Only if neither is true does the third
route open — appearance on the 503A bulks list. Three further conditions sit
alongside: the substance cannot be on the withdrawn-or-removed list, the
product cannot be a copy of a commercially available drug made in
inordinate amounts, and it cannot be one FDA identifies as demonstrably
difficult to compound.

THE BULKS LIST AND WHAT THE CATEGORIES ARE NOT
The 503A bulks list is codified at 21 C.F.R. 216.23 and holds six substances —
Brilliant Blue G, cantharidin, diphenylcyclopropenone,
N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, squaric acid dibutyl ester, thymol iodide — four
topical-only. No peptide is on it. Listing happens by notice-and-comment
rulemaking after FDA consults its advisory committee and balances four
criteria: chemical characterization, safety, historical compounding use, and
evidence of effectiveness. Even listing carries no endorsement — the
regulation states the data are inadequate to establish general recognition of
safety or effectiveness for any listed substance.

Categories 1, 2 and 3 are something else: enforcement-discretion postures
described in FDA guidance while nominations are evaluated. Category 1

THE RESEARCH-USE SUPPLY CHANNEL
Two provisions get invoked as though they authorized human use. Neither
does. "For Research Use Only. Not for use in diagnostic procedures." comes
from 21 C.F.R. 809.10(c), a labeling provision for in vitro diagnostic products in
the laboratory research phase; its effect is to excuse a shipment from IVD
labeling requirements. The drug-side analogue, 21 C.F.R. 312.160, permits
shipping an investigational drug without an IND for laboratory animal or in
vitro use if the label reads "Not for use in humans" — and obliges the shipper
to confirm the consignee runs such tests. FDA may terminate that
authorization where the drug goes to anything but bona fide investigation.
Imported material fares no better. An article is refused admission if it is
adulterated, misbranded, or in violation of the approval requirement, and
FDA states that personal importation is in most circumstances illegal. The
discretion FDA describes is conditioned on facts this market does not
present: no promotion to US residents, generally not more than a
three-month supply, and a named US-licensed treating physician.

PRESCRIBER-FACING EXPOSURE
Exposure runs on three independent tracks. Federal: introducing, receiving
or delivering an adulterated or misbranded drug in interstate commerce are
prohibited acts, punishable by up to a year's imprisonment without proof of
intent, rising to three years with a prior conviction or intent to defraud.
State: board authority rests on statutory definitions of unprofessional
conduct reaching gross negligence, repeated negligent acts, incompetence,
and assisting a violation of the practice act — none requiring that the
prescriber handled a product, and all reaching a referral to a vendor. Civil
standard-of-care and causation questions are treated at length in The Expert
Witness Pocket Guide.
1.    Document the identity of the article and its regulatory status in the chart,
      in those terms — not as off-label use of an approved drug.
2. Where a compounded preparation is used, record the pathway (503A or
   503B), the facility, and the certificate of analysis relied on.
3. Build consent around the elements FDA's research-consent regulation
   enumerates: that the therapy is not approved for the indication, the
   foreseeable risks, the approved alternatives, and what is available if injury
   occurs.
4. Record the clinical reasoning and monitoring plan; a board reviews
   reasoning, not footnotes.
5. Verify category and listing status at the time of prescribing — these lists
   changed materially in April and May 2026 alone.

PEPTIDES AND THE SECTION 503A BULKS LIST: STATUS AS OF
AUGUST 2026
Under section 503A of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, a bulk drug
substance may be used in compounding only if it meets a USP or NF
monograph, is a component of an FDA-approved drug, or appears on FDA's
503A Bulks List. Most research peptides satisfy none of the first two, so the
Bulks List decides whether they can lawfully be compounded at all.
FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee met on July 23-24, 2026
under Docket No. FDA-2025-N-6895, announced at 91 Fed. Reg. 20465 (Apr.
16, 2026). Seven peptide families were considered, each in free base and
acetate form, for a total of fourteen individual substances put to separate
votes:
— BPC-157 — reviewed for ulcerative colitis
— KPV — reviewed for wound healing and inflammatory conditions
— TB-500 — reviewed for wound healing
— MOTS-c — reviewed for obesity and osteoporosis
— Emideltide (delta sleep-inducing peptide, DSIP) — reviewed for opioid
  withdrawal, chronic insomnia, and narcolepsy
— Semax — reviewed for cerebral ischemia, migraine, and trigeminal
  neuralgia
— Epitalon — reviewed for insomnia
FDA review staff recommended against listing all fourteen. Each briefing
document concludes that a balancing of the four statutory criteria weighs
against placing the substance on the list. The grounds were consistent: the
substances are not well characterized chemically, nominations lacked
certificates of analysis and impurity, aggregate or endotoxin controls,
injected peptides carry immunogenicity risk, and approved drugs already
exist for every condition reviewed.

Cosmetic, compounded, or
supplement?
Three regulatory regimes reaching the same molecule, and what each
permits.

The same peptide can be sold under three very different regulatory regimes
depending on its route and the claims made — not its molecular identity. The
regime, not the molecule, determines what is lawful.

COSMETIC — TOPICAL, APPEARANCE CLAIMS ONLY
Under the FD&C Act and the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act
(MoCRA, 2022), a topical product that makes only appearance claims
(moisturizing, improving the look of fine lines) is a cosmetic: no premarket
approval, but safety and labeling rules apply. A therapeutic claim (healing,
regenerating tissue, treating a condition) reclassifies it as an unapproved
drug.
Example: a GHK-copper cream sold for skin appearance is a cosmetic; the
identical cream marketed to "regenerate tissue" crosses into drug territory.

COMPOUNDED — PRESCRIPTION, 503A / 503B
A licensed pharmacy may compound a peptide for an individual patient
prescription (503A) or as cGMP batches for clinics (503B). The bulk substance
must ordinarily satisfy a statutory pathway: an applicable USP or NF
monograph, a component of an FDA-approved drug, or the 503A Bulks List
itself. Separately from those routes, FDA’s interim policy describes
circumstances in which the agency does not intend to act on certain
Category 1 nominated substances while evaluation is pending — enforcement
posture, not statutory eligibility. A COA for identity, purity and sterility is
expected either way.
Example: GHK-copper sits in Category 1 for non-injectable routes, so FDA
does not currently intend to act against its use in compounding pending
evaluation. That is not the same as statutory eligibility, and most injectable
research peptides satisfy no pathway at all.

COSMETIC, COMPOUNDED, OR SUPPLEMENT? · CONTINUED

DIETARY SUPPLEMENT — ORAL, DSHEA
Marketing a peptide as an oral "dietary supplement" under DSHEA is the most
legally contested route. Most synthetic peptides do not meet the statutory
definition of a dietary ingredient and lack a New Dietary Ingredient (NDI)
notification; the FDA has stated that several — including BPC-157 — are
excluded from the supplement category and treated as unapproved drugs.
Example: oral BPC-157 capsules are widely sold as "supplements," but the FDA
has issued warning letters rejecting that classification.

  Bottom line: route plus claim define the regime — the same peptide can
  be a lawful cosmetic, a prescription compound, or an unlawful
  "supplement" depending on how it is sold.

TWO LEGAL PITFALLS FOR PROVIDERS
"Off-label" doesn't apply to unapproved drugs. Off-label use is a legal
pathway only for drugs holding at least one FDA-approved indication.
Prescribing BPC-157 or any never-approved peptide is use of an unapproved
new drug — IND, Expanded Access, and Right-to-Try do not cover routine
wellness use.
"Recommending" is not a liability shield: directing patients to source
research-grade peptides for self-administration is practicing medicine;
liability and board jurisdiction attach regardless of whether the practitioner
handled the product, and most malpractice policies exclude
unapproved-drug claims.

  Bottom line: if a peptide is not FDA-approved, you are operating outside
  the drug-approval framework — document the rationale, product source,
  and informed consent.

Case study: Lilly v. FDA
Retatrutide and the 40-amino-acid boundary, from complaint to
pending appeal.

Retatrutide and the >40-amino-acid boundary (2023–2025)

THE MOLECULE AND THE DISPUTE
Retatrutide (Lilly's investigational GLP-1/GIP/glucagon triple agonist) has a
39-alpha-amino-acid backbone plus a covalent side chain of one
gamma-glutamate and one ADO residue — the latter not an alpha amino acid.
Counted cumulatively it has 41 residues; counted as alpha amino acids, 39
(sequence patent-disclosed: US20230143781A1).
In November 2023 Lilly asked the FDA to classify it as a biological "protein"
under the BPCIA's >40-amino-acid threshold, counting all 41 residues. The
FDA counted only the 39 backbone alpha amino acids, excluded the ADO
residue, and ruled it a drug under the FD&C Act — declining to designate it
"analogous to a protein."

THE RULING AND WHY IT MATTERS
Lilly sued (Eli Lilly & Co. v. Kennedy, No. 1:24-cv-01503-TWP-KMB, S.D. Ind.).
The September 30, 2025 order split: the court upheld FDA's determination
that retatrutide does not meet the regulatory definition of a protein, but
vacated FDA's separate determination that it could not be "analogous to a
protein" and remanded that question to the agency. Final judgment entered
December 16, 2025. It did not establish that retatrutide is a biologic.
Lilly appealed in February 2026 (No. 26-1301, 7th Cir.), briefed through June
2026, and the appellate proceeding remains pending as of 13 August 2026. As
a single district-court ruling under appeal it is persuasive rather than settled
national law, but it is the cleanest case to date on how the >40-residue
threshold pivots on which residues you count — with large commercial
stakes: BLA vs NDA pathways, 12-year biologic vs shorter NCE exclusivity,
biosimilar vs generic competition, and very different 503A compounding
posture.

About this guide
The Peptide Pocket Guide is part of the Hormonaly Press reference list, a
series of clinically grounded books on hormone and peptide therapeutics. Its
companion volume, The Expert Witness Pocket Guide, addresses what
happens when this evidence — or its absence — is examined by a court.

METHOD
Entries were verified against primary literature, approved labels, trial
registries, and regulatory documents. GRADE ratings reflect the state of
published evidence as of the date below. Every reference was checked
against PubMed; non-indexed sources are explicitly flagged.

DISCLAIMER
This guide is a professional reference for licensed clinicians. It is not medical
advice, not a treatment protocol, and not a recommendation to prescribe any
molecule described in it. Regulatory status changes by administrative action,
sometimes within weeks; verify currency before relying on any status
statement here. Clinical decisions rest with the treating clinician.

CORRECTIONS
Errors reported to Hormonaly Press are corrected in the next printing and
acknowledged.
