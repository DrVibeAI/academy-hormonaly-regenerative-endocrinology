How to use this guide
This pocket guide is built for the clinician who wants a clear, current picture
of aesthetic and regenerative endocrinology — the optimization of skin, gut,
and the endocrine axes for healthspan — without wading through a textbook.
It moves in four parts: foundations, the organs of aging, interventions, and
the regenerative frontier with a framework for practice. It is designed to be
read in an afternoon or consulted at the point of care.
H O W E A C H C H A P T E R I S B U I L T
— A one-line statement of the chapter's clinical idea, then focused prose with
key terms in bold.
— Inline citations[1] point to a numbered References block at the foot of each
chapter. Numbering restarts at 1 in every chapter, so a citation always
refers to its own chapter's list, never a running total.
— Strength-of-evidence pills ( GRADE A GRADE B GRADE C GRADE D ) mark
how good the evidence is for the specific use described — not the elegance
of the biology, nor its marketing.
— Callout boxes carry the practical takeaway — and, in every chapter, clinical
pearls for both testing and therapy.
— A closing "three things to remember" strip distills each chapter to its core.
S T R E N G T H O F E V I D E N C E A T A G L A N C E
S C O P E & I N T E N T
Evidence grades reflect evidence quality for the specific indication discussed,
not biological plausibility or mechanistic elegance. This field attracts genuine
advances and considerable hype; the guide states plainly what is established,
what is a useful adjunct, and what remains investigational or unapproved. It is
a reference, not a protocol, and not medical advice for any individual — it
informs clinical reasoning but does not replace specialist input, formal
guidelines, or individualized judgment. Approvals, compounding rules, and
the evidence base change quickly: verify against current sources, and confirm
every citation against the primary literature, before acting.

Chapter 1 · What is regenerative
endocrinology?
Using endocrine biology to extend the years lived in good health — not
merely to chase a number.
Regenerative and aesthetic endocrinology applies the tools of endocrinology
— hormones, signaling peptides, nutrient-sensing pathways, and the cell
biology of repair — to the central goal of modern preventive medicine:
extending healthspan, the span of life spent free of chronic disease and
disability, rather than lifespan alone.[1] The aesthetic dimension is not vanity;
the skin is the most visible readout of systemic endocrine and metabolic
health, and the same processes that age it age the vasculature, the brain, and
the bone.
The field rests on a unifying insight from geroscience: aging is driven by a
finite set of interacting hallmarks — among them genomic instability,
epigenetic drift, loss of proteostasis, deregulated nutrient sensing,
mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem-cell exhaustion, altered
intercellular communication, and chronic inflammation.[2,3] Hormonal
decline both reflects and accelerates several of these. That is why an
endocrine lens is powerful — and why it must be disciplined.

Chapter 2 · The aging endocrine
system
Each axis declines on its own schedule — the "-pauses" of endocrine
aging.
Aging is not a uniform fade. Each endocrine axis follows a characteristic
trajectory, and recognizing them is the first clinical skill in this field.[1] The
somatopause is the progressive fall in growth-hormone pulse amplitude and
IGF-1, beginning in the third decade and among the steepest of all.[2]
Adrenopause is the parallel decline of DHEA and DHEA-sulfate, which by age
70–80 reach 10–20% of young-adult levels.[3] The andropause — better
termed late-onset hypogonadism — is a gradual decline in total and (more
steeply) free testosterone of approximately 0.5–2% per year, influenced by
adiposity, illness, and rising SHBG.[4]
The female menopause is the exception: not a gentle slope but a cliff, with
the loss of ovarian estradiol and progesterone over the perimenopausal
transition, formally staged by STRAW+10.[5] Thyropause is subtler — TSH
distributions shift upward with age, and modest subclinical changes are
common and often benign in the very old. Melatonin amplitude and the
robustness of cortisol's diurnal rhythm also blunt with age, themes returned
to in Chapter 4.

Chapter 3 · Peptides &
bioregulators
What signaling peptides actually are — and a hard line between
mechanism and proof.
Peptides are short amino-acid chains that act as signaling molecules:
hormones (insulin, GLP-1), releasing factors (GHRH), and local repair and
growth factors. The growth-hormone axis is the prototype — GHRH and
ghrelin stimulate, somatostatin inhibits, and synthetic secretagogues (GHRH
analogs like CJC-1295; ghrelin-receptor agonists like ipamorelin) act on these
receptors to raise pulsatile GH.[1,2] The mechanistic plausibility is genuine;
long-term human evidence in healthy aging is not.
The "bioregulator" tradition — short peptides such as epitalon (pineal) and
thymalin (thymic), associated with Khavinson — proposes tissue-specific
gene-regulatory effects.[3] The mechanistic literature is intriguing, but the
human evidence is sparse, largely small and from a single research lineage,
and falls short of supporting routine use. No adequately powered,
multicenter randomized trials demonstrating improved healthspan or
lifespan exist.
Regulatory status reflects June 2026 and may change; verify current FDA
guidance before prescribing.
R E G U L A T O R Y R E A L I T Y — V E R I F Y B E F O R E P R E S C R I B I N G ( S T A T U S
J U N E 2 0 2 6 )
As of June 2026, the U.S. FDA peptide-compounding landscape remains
unsettled. Several popular peptides have appeared on, been withdrawn from,
or are now under review in relation to the 503A bulk-substance lists: a 2023
round placed many in Category 2 (restricted), and in 2026 the FDA withdrew
several from Category 2 and referred seven — BPC-157, KPV, TB-500,
MOTS-c, DSIP (emideltide), semax, and epitalon — to its Pharmacy
Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) for a 23–24 July 2026 meeting.
Crucially, nomination, withdrawal from Category 2, or PCAC review is not
FDA approval and does not by itself establish a settled lawful clinical
compounding route; "research use only" is not a clinical loophole, and topical
GHK-Cu (with limits) is the narrow Category 1 exception. Verify the current
FDA 503A/503B lists, PCAC outcomes, and your state-board rules before
clinical use.[4]

Chapter 4 · Circadian &
neuroendocrine timing
When a hormone is secreted matters as much as how much — and
timing is modifiable.
The suprachiasmatic nucleus entrains a body-wide network of molecular
clocks that gate metabolism, hormone secretion, and tissue repair across the
24-hour day.[1] Cortisol peaks near waking; growth hormone and most tissue
repair are nocturnal and sleep-dependent; melatonin rises in darkness;
insulin sensitivity is highest in the morning.[2] Aging blunts the amplitude of
these rhythms — flatter cortisol curves, lower melatonin, fragmented
slow-wave sleep — and that flattening is itself a driver of metabolic and
immune decline.
Two levers are both potent and free. Light is the dominant zeitgeber:
ordinary evening room light meaningfully suppresses and delays melatonin,
degrading sleep quality and downstream repair.[3] The morning is the mirror
image: stepping into outdoor light soon after waking — ideally within an hour
— advances and stabilizes the central clock, the strongest daily anchor
available.[4] Because the entraining signal is ocular — melanopsin-containing
retinal cells that project to the SCN — the light must reach the eyes (go
outside rather than rely on glass; never look straight at the sun). Letting it fall
on the body and skin is a sensible bonus — the skin carries its own peripheral
clocks, and morning time outdoors supports vitamin D and mood — but it
does not replace ocular light, and it is no license for unprotected midday UV.
Meal timing is the second: eating in alignment with the active phase — for
example early time-restricted eating — improves insulin sensitivity and blood
pressure even without weight loss.[5] Circadian alignment is the substrate on
which every other intervention in this guide is built.
CLINICAL PEARL — Before adding any peptide, hormone, or supplement
for "fatigue" or "poor recovery," fix the circadian basics first: consistent
wake time, bright morning light, dim evening light, and a protected sleep
window. Many presentations resolve here — at no cost and no risk.

Chapter 5 · The skin as an
endocrine organ
The most visible readout of systemic endocrine and metabolic aging.
Skin aging proceeds on two tracks. Intrinsic (chronological) aging thins the
epidermis and dermis, reduces collagen by roughly 1% per year, fragments
the elastic network, and lowers fibroblast output — changes first quantified
decades ago.[1] Extrinsic aging, dominated by ultraviolet exposure, drives
matrix metalloproteinase activation that degrades collagen faster than it is
replaced, producing the coarse wrinkling and dyspigmentation of
photoaging; glycation and smoking compound it.[2]
Crucially, the skin is not a passive recipient of hormones — it is a peripheral
endocrine organ that expresses receptors for, and locally synthesizes, a
broad range of signaling molecules, and performs its own
steroidogenesis.[3,4] The table summarizes the major circulating inputs; the
sections that follow add the detail and the locally produced peptides that the
table cannot hold.

S T E R O I D H O R M O N E S
Estrogens are probably the single most influential class for skin quality: they
stimulate fibroblast collagen synthesis, raise dermal thickness and hyaluronic
acid content, support barrier hydration, and accelerate wound healing, so the
post-menopausal estrogen drop drives much of the thinning, dryness, and
lost elasticity of aging skin.[5,6] Androgens — testosterone and, more
potently, DHT generated locally by 5α-reductase — enlarge sebaceous glands
and raise sebum, making them central to acne, seborrhea, hirsutism, and
androgenetic alopecia; the skin is itself a site of androgen metabolism.[4]
Glucocorticoid excess thins the dermis, suppresses collagen, impairs healing,
and produces striae, through both systemic cortisol and a local cutaneous
HPA-type axis — relevant to Cushing's and to chronic stress alike (Chapter 8).
The hormonal form of vitamin D (calcitriol) governs keratinocyte
proliferation and differentiation and modulates cutaneous immunity, and its
synthesis begins in the skin under UVB;[7] DHEA and progesterone play more
modest, partly precursor-driven roles.
T H Y R O I D H O R M O N E S
T3 and T4 govern epidermal turnover, sebum and sweat secretion, and hair
cycling. Hypothyroidism gives dry, coarse, cool skin and myxedema;
hyperthyroidism gives warm, moist skin and, in Graves' disease, pretibial
myxedema.[8]
P E P T I D E A N D P R O T E I N H O R M O N E S
Growth hormone and IGF-1 promote fibroblast proliferation, collagen
synthesis, and dermal thickness; IGF-1 also stimulates sebaceous activity,
part of why high-glycemic, high-insulin states track with acne,[9] while
marked insulin resistance surfaces cutaneously as acanthosis nigricans. The
melanocortins α-MSH and ACTH — both POMC-derived — drive
melanogenesis through MC1R and are anti-inflammatory in skin.[3] Prolactin
and oxytocin have cutaneous receptors with roles in hair-follicle biology,
sebaceous function, and wound healing,[10,11] and locally produced PTHrP
regulates keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation.[4]

A large amount of skin biology runs on paracrine and autocrine peptides
rather than circulating hormones: EGF and FGF7/KGF for keratinocyte
proliferation, TGF-β for fibroblast activation and scarring, VEGF for
angiogenesis, and PDGF for wound repair.[12] Neuropeptides such as
substance P and CGRP mediate neurogenic inflammation, itch, and
vasodilation,[13] while the antimicrobial peptides cathelicidin (LL-37) and the
defensins anchor barrier immunity — with LL-37 specifically implicated in
rosacea.[14,15] Locally synthesized melatonin and β-endorphin add
antioxidant and stress-response layers.[3]
W H A T T H E E V I D E N C E S U P P O R T S
Among topical agents, retinoids have the strongest dermatologic evidence for
reversing measurable signs of photoaging and remain first-line GRADE A. For
systemic menopausal hormone therapy, skin benefit is real but secondary —
never an indication on its own.[6] Oral collagen peptides show modest
improvements in hydration, elasticity, and wrinkle parameters in randomized
trials, though most are small and industry-funded GRADE C,[16] and topical
GHK-Cu has decades of mechanistic and small-trial support for collagen
stimulation GRADE C.[17] The familiar "cosmetic peptides" borrow directly
from this endogenous signaling: signal peptides such as palmitoyl
pentapeptide-4 (Matrixyl) and copper tripeptide GHK-Cu target matrix and
collagen, and neuromodulatory acetyl hexapeptide-8 (Argireline) softens
expression lines — generally with modest, small-trial evidence GRADE C.[18]

Although not a formally recognized subspecialty, these threads are
increasingly discussed together under the label cosmetic (aesthetic)
endocrinology — a descriptive framing for a familiar clinical activity. In
practice it means treating endocrine pathophysiology to improve the
cosmetically bothersome findings patients present with — most prominently
the hirsutism, androgenetic alopecia, and acne of PCOS and
hyperandrogenism.[19,20] The skin is its organ, and a growing literature
frames the skin explicitly as an endocrine tissue that both produces and
responds to hormones.[21]
The estrogen–skin axis is the best-developed lever. Because sex steroids are
small lipophilic molecules, they can be delivered topically for local effect with
limited systemic exposure;[20] oral or topical estrogen slows — though it
cannot reverse — post-menopausal skin aging, improving collagen, thickness,
and hydration, and selective estrogen-receptor modulators (SERMs) are
being explored to capture that benefit more selectively.[22,23] The axis also
runs through stress: psychological stress, acting via the HPA axis and
androgens, is associated with acne — a reminder that a cosmetic complaint is
often a systemic readout.[24]
The lever cuts both ways. Because each of these findings is downstream of a
hormonal driver, treating the endocrine cause is often the most durable
cosmetic intervention — more so than a topical measure applied to the
surface alone. Suppressing ovarian and adrenal androgen output (a combined
oral contraceptive, spironolactone, or 5α-reductase inhibition) improves the
acne, seborrhea, and hirsutism of hyperandrogenism; restoring euthyroidism
reverses the dry, coarse skin and diffuse shedding of hypothyroidism; and
correcting hypercortisolism resolves the dermal thinning and easy bruising it
drives. The discipline of the field is to read the skin as a presenting sign,
identify the axis behind it, and treat that axis to its guideline target — rather
than chase the visible finding in isolation.
CLINICAL PEARL — THE AESTHETIC EXAM IS A SYSTEMIC SCREEN —
Sudden skin thinning and easy bruising suggest cortisol excess; coarse,
dry, cool skin suggests hypothyroidism; new acanthosis nigricans flags
insulin resistance; rapid photoaging tracks UV, glycation, and smoking.
Read the skin as a window on the whole endocrine system before
reaching for a serum.

Chapter 6 · The skin as a
neuro–immuno–endocrine organ
From a single endocrine tissue to an integrated sensor that tunes
systemic homeostasis.
The previous chapter treated the skin as an endocrine organ. The fuller
picture, consolidated in recent reviews, is that the skin is a
neuro–immuno–endocrine (NIE) organ: a peripheral system that senses the
environment, mounts a local hormonal and immune response, and — through
hormones, neuropeptides, and primed immune cells entering the circulation,
or through sensory nerves signaling the brain — helps adjust systemic
homeostasis and allostasis.[1] Some authors order the triad differently
(neuro–endocrine–immune), stressing that the immune compartment often
delivers the final phenotype, but the unifying claim is the same: the skin
computes environmental information and reports it inward.[2] This is not a
fringe idea — the hypothesis that the skin is a self-contained neuroendocrine
organ, articulated decades ago, has since been validated experimentally
across cell types and species.[3] What follows is the mechanistic foundation,
framed deliberately as biology rather than a menu of interventions.

The skin’s machinery is compartmentalized into four immuno–endocrine
units — epidermal, dermal, hypodermal, and adnexal (the pilosebaceous unit
and glands) — that can act alone or in concert.[1] Each contains resident cells
with both hormone-making and immune capacity (keratinocytes,
melanocytes, fibroblasts, sebocytes, adipocytes, mast cells, dendritic and
Langerhans cells), wired together by sensory nerves that penetrate to the
stratum corneum. Communication runs three ways: soluble factors between
neighboring cells, rapid neural transmission, and release into the circulation
that can reach distant organs.
A S T E R O I D O G E N I C O R G A N , N O T J U S T A C O R T I S O L S I T E
Chapter 8 develops the cutaneous glucocorticoid axis; the broader fact is that
human skin is a competent steroidogenic organ. Skin cells express CYP11A1,
the rate-limiting enzyme that cleaves cholesterol to pregnenolone, and can
build glucocorticoids, androgens, and estrogens locally — or activate
precursors delivered from the blood.[4] Local interconversion uses familiar
enzymes: 11β-HSD1/2 for cortisol, 5α-reductase for dihydrotestosterone, and
17β-HSD for sex-steroid steps. Independent work shows
keratinocyte-derived, de novo glucocorticoids actively police local immune
tone rather than merely echoing systemic cortisol.[5]

In most individuals, cutaneous synthesis is the dominant natural source of

signals oral vitamin D cannot reproduce — though most of these metabolites
remain preclinical, and none of this licenses unprotected sun exposure.

hair cycle, and mitochondrial function,[7] and a local somatotropic loop
(growth hormone and dermally produced IGF-1) influences dermal thickness,
wound healing, and the hair follicle.[8]

On the neural side, sensory nerves releasing substance P and CGRP drive
neurogenic inflammation and form the cutaneous arm of the brain–skin axis;
the skin also makes melatonin and serotonin locally, with melatonin acting as
a cutaneous antioxidant. On the immune side, skin-associated lymphoid
tissue and keratinocyte cytokines do not stay local. The clearest healthspan
link is the inflammatory skin “march”: chronic psoriatic inflammation is
associated with insulin resistance and cardiometabolic disease, and the
atopic march propagates allergic disease systemically.[10] Skin inflammation,
in other words, is a plausible contributor to the same metabolic and vascular
aging this guide targets elsewhere.
T H E H A I R F O L L I C L E : A N E N D O C R I N E M I N I - O R G A N
The pilosebaceous unit is the most autonomous of the adnexal structures —
in effect a miniature endocrine organ. The human hair follicle runs functional
peripheral equivalents of the central axes: it synthesizes cortisol and
operates a local HPA-like loop,[11] and ex vivo it sustains a
hypothalamic–pituitary–somatotropic equivalent (GHRH, GH, IGF-1) that
paces its own growth.[12] It is also an immune-privileged site — normally
shielded from immune attack — and collapse of that privilege drives alopecia
areata, now treatable with JAK inhibitors that interrupt the responsible
cytokine signaling (oral baricitinib is approved for severe disease GRADE
A).[13]
Hormones set the follicle’s tempo. Thyroid hormone and prolactin tune the
cycle and estrogen withdrawal at menopause thins hair, but the dominant
clinical lever is androgenic: 5α-reductase converts testosterone to DHT,
which paradoxically miniaturizes genetically susceptible scalp follicles
(androgenetic alopecia) while driving beard and body hair. This is why
5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride) and androgen-independent minoxidil
are the mainstays of pattern hair loss, and why a sudden physiologic stressor
or thyroid shift can tip follicles into telogen effluvium.
D I S E A S E M O D E L · T H E T R I N I T Y I N O N E C O N D I T I O N
Rosacea integrates all three arms: immune sensing (TLR activation;
cathelicidin cleaved to LL-37), an endocrine coordinator (CRH, inducible by
even physiological UVB), and a neurovascular trigger (TRP channels,
substance P, CGRP, mast-cell degranulation) — converging on flushing,
inflammation, and angiogenesis.[2,14]

T H E S K I N M I C R O B I O M E — T H E F O U R T H I N T E R F A C E
These units do not operate in isolation: they sit in constant dialogue with a
resident microbiome. Sebaceous sites are dominated by Cutibacterium
acnes; moist and dry sites by Staphylococcus epidermidis and other
commensals that educate cutaneous immunity, produce antimicrobial
metabolites, and help hold the barrier in homeostasis GRADE B.[15] These
organisms act as sentinels — in balance they restrain pathogens, but they can
shift to opportunists, form biofilms, and tip into the dysbiosis seen in acne
and atopic dermatitis GRADE B.[16]
With age the community itself changes: commensal diversity falls and
Cutibacterium declines as sebum output drops — a microbial parallel to
inflammaging that helps explain the drier, more reactive barrier of older
skin.[16]
The clinical caveat is the usual one — the biology is real, but the “probiotic
skincare” and live-bacterial-spray market runs well ahead of controlled
outcome data GRADE C; treat the microbiome as an interface to respect (go
easy on antiseptics and harsh surfactants), not yet a reliable therapeutic
target.
Recent work sharpens this picture: metagenomic studies of aging skin show
Cutibacterium acnes dominance falling and overall diversity rising, a shift
that tracks measurably with reduced elasticity and more wrinkles,[17] and
large multisite cohorts confirm the pattern across body sites — to the point
that machine-learning models now estimate chronological age from the skin
microbiome.[18]
The thread running through this chapter is integration: the skin does not
merely host these systems side by side but runs them as one circuit, so a
signal entering at any node — ultraviolet light, a shift in the microbiome, a
surge of stress hormone, a breach of the barrier — propagates to the others
and, often, inward to the body. That is why a single cutaneous complaint can
carry endocrine, neural, immune, and microbial contributions at once, and
why durable results usually come from addressing the upstream driver rather
than any one downstream layer. The practical inference is modest but firm:
read the skin as an information-rich readout of systemic state, respect its
interfaces — barrier, microbiome, and circadian and stress inputs — before
reaching for actives, and keep the mechanistic richness of this biology clearly
separate from the far thinner evidence for intervening on it directly.

Chapter 7 · The endocrine atlas of
skin
A single-cell atlas of the skin's endocrine signaling network — and how it
senesces with age.
Chapter 5 framed the skin as both a hormone target and a hormone
producer. A cellular-resolution map now lets us infer that activity cell by cell
— as transcriptomic predictions (which cells express the machinery to make
or sense a hormone), not direct measurements of secretion or function. The
Hormone Cell Atlas is a landmark transcriptomic resource; its cell-level
findings represent predicted endocrine programs that require functional
validation, and the clinical inferences below should be read in that light. The
Hormone Cell Atlas profiled 379 hormone and receptor genes across roughly
14 million single cells from 47 human tissues — skin among them — and
scored each cell type's predicted hormone-producing and
hormone-receiving strength.[1] Two of its findings reframe how we should
think about skin.
First, endocrine identity tracks cellular lineage more than tissue of origin,
and the capacity is nearly universal: roughly 74% of cell types were predicted
to produce, and 93% to respond to, at least one hormone.[1] Second, it
follows that the skin — the body's largest organ, built from every major
lineage (epithelial, mesenchymal, endothelial, adipocyte, immune, neural) in
vast quantity — is plausibly one of the body's largest distributed endocrine
signaling networks — an inference from transcriptomic prediction rather
than direct measurement of secretion.[2] It is also endocrine-autonomous in
ways few tissues are: it is the body's site of vitamin D synthesis,[3] and it runs
a local, fully competent HPA-like axis — producing CRH, cleaving POMC to
ACTH, α-MSH and β-endorphin, and synthesizing cortisol in situ.[4]
The scale argument is straightforward. At roughly 1.5–2 m² of surface and up
to 15% of body weight once the subcutis is counted, the skin holds a far larger
population of hormone-competent cells than the compact classical glands —
the pituitary, adrenal, and thyroid are each measured in grams. What it lacks
in concentrated, high-amplitude output it makes up in distributed scale,
anatomical reach, and the autonomy to both make and respond to its own
hormonal signals.[2]

The lineages this atlas maps do not age evenly. With chronological time and
cumulative UV exposure, dermal fibroblasts, keratinocytes, and endothelial

active, broadcasting a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) of

“altered intercellular communication” hallmark — blunting neighboring cells'
responsiveness to estrogen, IGF-1, and retinoids, while estrogen loss at
menopause is itself associated with a heavier senescent burden and slower
fibroblast turnover.[4,6] The practical lesson is that hormone optimization
and senescence are not separate levers: correcting a true deficiency works
partly by easing the senescent, pro-inflammatory state these endocrine cells
fall into. Senolytic and senomorphic strategies to clear or quiet them (Ch. 17)
are an active dermatologic frontier, though skin-specific human evidence is
still early.

Chapter 8 · The skin–glucocorticoid
axis
Cortisol is written on the skin — the “cortisol face,” and how to read and
reset the diurnal rhythm.
The skin is not only a target of cortisol but a local glucocorticoid factory.
Keratinocytes and fibroblasts express the glucocorticoid receptor, run a
peripheral HPA-like axis (CRH → POMC → ACTH → cortisol), and regenerate
active cortisol in situ through 11β-HSD1 — an enzyme whose cutaneous
activity rises with age, amplifying local glucocorticoid exposure just as the
skin is least able to tolerate it.[1,2] Sustained glucocorticoid excess is visibly
catabolic: it suppresses fibroblast collagen synthesis, thins the dermis and
epidermis, delays barrier and wound repair, and produces the easy bruising,
striae, and atrophy familiar from topical-steroid overuse.[3] Chronic
psychological stress drives the same biology through the brain–skin axis,
accelerating barrier dysfunction, inflammation, and visible aging.[4]
T H E “ C O R T I S O L F A C E ”
The popular term “cortisol face” describes facial puffiness, rounding, and
fluid retention blamed on stress and high cortisol. Hold two truths at once.
The genuine endocrine entity is the moon facies of Cushing’s syndrome — a
rounded, plethoric face with dorsocervical and supraclavicular fat pads,
central adiposity, wide purple striae, proximal muscle weakness, and easy
bruising — a referable diagnosis, not a cosmetic state.[5] Most of what
circulates online as “cortisol face,” however, is not Cushing’s: it is the
lifestyle-and-fluid picture of poor sleep, alcohol, high salt, and inflammation.
The clinician’s task is to reassure the many while screening the few who carry
red flags.

cortisol, or a 1 mg overnight dexamethasone-suppression test — and refer to
endocrinology.[5] Consumer “cortisol panels” (random serum or hair cortisol)
are poorly validated for the worried-well; resist over-testing.

Chapter 9 · Estrogen and skin aging
The hormone whose withdrawal most visibly ages the skin — and what
the evidence supports doing about it.
If one hormone dominates the visible aging of women's skin, it is estrogen. Its
decline at menopause opens a brief, steep, and largely quantifiable phase of
skin aging that overlies the slow background of chronological change — and
much of it can be partly reversed by restoring estrogen, though, as below,
skin benefit is never on its own a reason to prescribe.[1]
W H A T E S T R O G E N D O E S I N T H E S K I N
Skin is a broad estrogen target. It expresses estrogen receptors —
predominantly ERβ, with ERα and the membrane receptor GPER — on
keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and hair-follicle and sebaceous cells. Through
them estrogen drives fibroblast synthesis of type I and III collagen and
hyaluronic acid, maintains dermal thickness and water content, supports the
barrier, speeds wound healing, and modulates pigmentation.[1,2] Withdrawal
of that signal is what leaves estrogen-deficient skin thinner, drier, less elastic,
and slower to heal.
T H E M E N O P A U S E I N F L E C T I O N
The decline is not gradual. Skin collagen falls fastest in the first
postmenopausal years — on the order of 30% within the first five years, then
roughly 2% per year — so dermal aging tracks years since menopause more
tightly than chronological age.[1,3] Elasticity and hydration fall on a similar
curve, which is why the perimenopausal transition is so often when patients
first notice an abrupt change.
Two features make this phase clinically distinctive. First, it is partly separable
from photoaging: where ultraviolet damage concentrates on sun-exposed
surfaces and coarsens them, estrogen-related change is more uniform —
generalized thinning, dryness, and loss of firmness across exposed and
covered sites alike. Second, it is most responsive to intervention early, while
the dermal architecture is still relatively intact, which is why the
perimenopausal window is when counseling on sun protection, barrier care,
and — where indicated for other reasons — hormone therapy has the most to
work with. Recognizing the pattern reframes the "sudden aging" patients
report as a predictable, time-limited inflection rather than an inevitable slide.

What gets absorbed through the skin — separating endocrine evidence

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) are exogenous compounds that
interfere with hormone synthesis, transport, signaling, or metabolism —
often by mimicking or blocking estrogen, androgen, or thyroid receptors.[1,2]
Because the skin is a large absorptive surface in daily contact with
personal-care products, cosmetics are one relevant route of exposure. The
classes that recur in this conversation are well characterized: phthalates

oxybenzone; triclosan; and certain bisphenols and PFAS. The mechanistic
concern is genuine, and biomonitoring shows exposure is near-universal at
the population level.

The honest position sits between dismissal and alarm. Absorption is

though absorption is not the same as demonstrated harm.[5] At the same

clinical endpoints from cosmetic-level exposure remain uncertain, with
low-dose and mixture effects still debated.[6] Professional bodies therefore
counsel precaution weighted toward vulnerable windows — pregnancy,
infancy, and puberty — rather than blanket avoidance.[1]
" C L E A N B E A U T Y " — U S E F U L C O R E , M A R K E T I N G S H E L L
"Clean beauty" is a marketing term, not a regulated standard: the FDA does
not define "clean" or "natural," and — even under the 2022 Modernization of
Cosmetics Regulation Act, which added facility registration, adverse-event
reporting, and safety substantiation — most cosmetics and ingredients still
reach the market without premarket approval.[7] "Chemical-free" is a
misnomer, and "natural" does not mean safe — botanical extracts are
common sensitizers. The defensible core of the movement is narrow and
reasonable: reduce unnecessary exposure to a few well-characterized
disruptors, particularly fragrance and certain preservatives, and especially
around pregnancy. The trap is letting EDC anxiety crowd out the
interventions that actually protect the skin.

in front. Daily sun protection and retinoids have strong evidence for skin
health; harm from cosmetic-level EDCs is plausible but unproven. If a
patient is worried, the high-yield moves are simple: favor fragrance-free

Chapter 11 · Adipose tissue as an
endocrine organ
The body's largest hormone-secreting organ — a metabolic command
center, and a quiet driver of skin and metabolic aging.
For most of the twentieth century, fat was treated as inert ballast. It is now
recognized as the body's largest endocrine organ by mass, secreting dozens
of adipokines that regulate appetite, insulin sensitivity, immunity,
reproduction, and vascular tone.[1] The turning point came in 1994 with the
cloning of leptin — the product of the ob (LEP) gene — which proved that
adipocytes signal directly to the brain.[2] Crucially, adipose tissue is not one
organ but several: subcutaneous, visceral, and a thin dermal layer wrapped
around the skin's appendages, each with distinct behavior.
T H E M A J O R A D I P O K I N E S
Leptin signals energy sufficiency to the hypothalamus, suppressing appetite
and permitting reproduction and thyroid activity; in obesity its levels are
high, but the brain becomes leptin-resistant, so the satiety signal fails.[2]
Adiponectin is the mirror image — insulin-sensitizing and anti-inflammatory
through AMPK, it is paradoxically low in obesity, and that fall tracks with
insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular risk.[3] Resistin links
adiposity to insulin resistance clearly in rodents, though its role in humans
remains debated. Overlaid on all of this, an enlarged, stressed fat mass
recruits macrophages and secretes TNF-α and IL-6 — the molecular bridge
from excess adiposity to the chronic inflammation and insulin resistance of
inflammaging (Chapter 13).

Where fat sits matters more than how much there is. Subcutaneous fat is a
relatively safe sink; visceral and ectopic fat — in liver, muscle, pericardium,
and pancreas — are metabolically toxic, driving insulin resistance and
secreting the inflammatory adipokines above. The personal fat threshold
concept explains why two people at the same BMI can differ so sharply:
metabolic disease appears when an individual's safe storage capacity is
exceeded and lipid spills into ectopic sites, not at any universal weight.
A D I P O S E A N D T H E S K I N
The skin carries its own fat. Dermal white adipose tissue (dWAT) is a distinct
adipocyte layer in the reticular dermis that is far more than insulation: it
cycles with the hair follicle, fuels wound healing, and mounts an active
antimicrobial defense — reactive adipogenesis releases cathelicidin to fight
Staphylococcus aureus.[4] With age, dWAT becomes dysfunctional: its
signaling shifts, hair-follicle support wanes, and a pro-fibrotic
adipocyte–myofibroblast transition contributes to scarring and the loss of
dermal resilience.[5] At the same time, loss of subcutaneous facial fat is one
of the least-appreciated drivers of the aged face — deflation and descent of
discrete fat compartments, not just wrinkles, produce the hollowing that
fillers and fat grafting aim to reverse.
T H E G L P - 1 E R A
Incretin therapy has made adipose biology unavoidable. GLP-1 and dual
GIP/GLP-1 agonists (Chapter 12) shrink fat mass and improve the adipokine
profile — raising adiponectin and lowering inflammatory tone, with
cardiovascular-outcome benefit now shown in large trials such as SELECT[6]
— but rapid, large-volume fat loss also depletes the subcutaneous and dermal
fat that gives the face its youthful contour, the phenomenon popularly called
"Ozempic face." It is a vivid reminder that adipose tissue is not only a
metabolic liability but a structural and endocrine asset; the aim of
regenerative practice is the right fat, in the right place, signaling correctly.

Chapter 12 · The gut–endocrine axis
By cell number, the body's largest endocrine organ — and the gateway to
inflammaging.
The gut is the body's largest endocrine surface. Enteroendocrine L- and
K-cells secrete incretins — GLP-1, GIP, and PYY — that govern insulin release,
gastric emptying, and satiety; this physiology underlies the most
consequential metabolic drugs of the decade.[1] The GLP-1 receptor agonist
semaglutide and the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide produce
double-digit weight loss with broad cardiometabolic benefit GRADE A,
repositioning the gut hormones at the center of healthspan medicine.[2,3]
The gut is also an endocrine interface. The microbiome modulates host
hormones — most strikingly through the estrobolome, the bacterial
β-glucuronidase activity that deconjugates and recirculates estrogens,
influencing systemic estrogen exposure.[4] When the barrier fails, microbial
products such as lipopolysaccharide cross into the circulation, driving the
low-grade, systemic inflammation ("metabolic endotoxemia") that links a
leaky gut to insulin resistance and inflammaging.[5]
CLINICAL PEARL — A GLP-1 agonist is an endocrine, not merely a
"weight-loss," intervention — it should be prescribed and monitored as
one, with attention to lean-mass preservation (protein and resistance
training), gastrointestinal tolerance, and the established cautions. The
regenerative payoff of gut health is mostly indirect: lower inflammatory
tone and better insulin sensitivity.

Chapter 13 · Inflammaging &
metabolic aging
The shared engine beneath endocrine decline — and the target most
interventions actually hit.
Inflammaging is the chronic, sterile, low-grade inflammation that
accumulates with age and predicts frailty, multimorbidity, and mortality.[1] It
is fed by several hallmarks at once: cellular senescence, in which
growth-arrested cells secrete a pro-inflammatory "SASP" that damages
neighbors and, in mouse models, measurably shortens healthy lifespan when
allowed to accumulate;[2] mitochondrial dysfunction; and dysbiosis (Chapter
12). These converge on insulin resistance — arguably the central metabolic
lesion of aging.[3]
Nutrient-sensing pathways tie this together. Chronic overnutrition keeps
mTOR and IGF-1 signaling high and AMPK (the fasting/energy-stress sensor)
low — a pro-growth, pro-aging posture. The two most-studied
pharmacologic levers act here: rapamycin inhibits mTOR and extends
lifespan even when started late in mice,[4] and metformin activates AMPK
and is the basis of the TAME trial's attempt to test aging itself as an
indication.[5] Both remain investigational for healthy-aging use in humans.
WHY THIS CHAPTER MATTERS — Most "regenerative" interventions —
exercise, time-restricted eating, GLP-1 agonists, senolytics, omega-3 —
converge on the same final common path: lowering inflammatory tone
and restoring insulin sensitivity. Judge any new therapy by whether it
plausibly moves these needles, and whether that has been shown in
humans.

G L Y C A T I O N & A G E S — T H E S U G A R – A G I N G B R I D G E
One metabolic process ties skin, vascular, and endocrine aging together:
glycation. Reducing sugars react non-enzymatically with protein amino
groups to form a reversible Schiff base, then a more stable Amadori product,
and finally irreversible advanced glycation end products (AGEs) —
accelerated by hyperglycemia, a high-sugar or heavily processed diet, and UV
exposure.[7] AGEs damage tissue two ways. Structurally, they covalently
cross-link long-lived collagen and elastin, stiffening the dermis and arterial
wall and producing the loss of elasticity that reads as aged skin GRADE B.
Biochemically, AGEs engage their receptor RAGE, driving NF-κB–mediated
release of IL-6, TNF-α, and matrix metalloproteinases — a direct molecular
link from metabolic load to inflammaging and matrix breakdown GRADE
B.[7,8]
The clinical levers follow the biology: glycemic control, a lower dietary-AGE
pattern (less high-heat, ultra-processed food), and photoprotection. Topical
and oral "anti-glycation" actives are marketed heavily but rest mostly on
surrogate endpoints such as skin autofluorescence rather than hard
outcomes GRADE C.[7]
These ideas rest on a now-classic literature: Franceschi's inflamm-aging
hypothesis,[9] the life-course mapping of chronic inflammation onto
age-related disease,[10] and Campisi's establishment of cellular senescence
and its secretory phenotype as central drivers.[11]

Chapter 14 · Hormone optimization
Replace confirmed, symptomatic deficiency to a physiologic range — not
beyond it.
The governing principle of regenerative endocrinology is restoration, not
supraphysiology: treat documented, symptomatic deficiency, dose to a
midnormal physiologic range for the patient's age and sex, and monitor.
"More is better" is the error that gives the field its bad name.
T E S T O S T E R O N E ( M E N )
For men with symptoms plus two low morning total testosterone
measurements, therapy improves sexual function, lean mass, mood, and bone
density GRADE A.[1] The TRAVERSE trial found testosterone non-inferior to
placebo for major adverse cardiovascular events in higher-risk men, while
signaling more atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, and acute kidney
injury.[2] Monitor testosterone, hematocrit (hold for >54%), PSA, and
symptoms; avoid if near-term fertility is desired.
E S T R O G E N & P R O G E S T E R O N E ( W O M E N )
For bothersome menopausal symptoms, hormone therapy is the most
effective treatment, and for healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of
menopause the benefits generally outweigh the risks GRADE A.[3]
Transdermal estradiol carries lower thrombotic risk than oral; a progestogen
is required for endometrial protection in women with a uterus. It is not
approved as a primary anti-aging or cardioprotective intervention —
symptom relief and bone protection are the indications.
T H Y R O I D , D H E A , A N D G R O W T H H O R M O N E
Overt hypothyroidism is treated; in older adults, subclinical hypothyroidism
rarely benefits from levothyroxine, which produced no symptom
improvement in the TRUST trial GRADE A — a caution against over-treating
mildly elevated TSH in aging.[4] DHEA replacement is biochemically logical
but disappointing in practice: a rigorous two-year trial found no benefit on
body composition, physical performance, or quality of life GRADE B.[5]
Growth hormone in healthy older adults is not recommended — modest
body-composition changes are outweighed by edema, arthralgia, insulin
resistance, and carpal tunnel, and it is illegal to prescribe for anti-aging in the
US GRADE A.[6]

Two practical points round out the picture. First, a genuine safety signal:
because research-use-only powder is made outside GMP, impurity,
inaccurate dosing, and heavy-metal contamination (arsenic, lead) are
documented. New hyperpigmentation, melanosis, or dark spots appearing on

(cutaneous hyperpigmentation is a classic sign of arsenic exposure) or
overdosing of growth- or melanocortin-pathway peptides, not a benign
cosmetic effect — and should prompt stopping the product and questioning
its source. Second, local reactions: injectable GHK-Cu commonly causes
injection-site irritation and inflammation (copper is locally irritating);
standard measures help — rotate sites, lower the concentration or volume,
apply a cold compress, and pause if persistent. Some users anecdotally inject
KPV (an anti-inflammatory tripeptide) at the inflamed site, and others report
dandruff or seborrheic scaling improving on the GLOW stack — both
anecdotal, unvalidated, and not tested indications. None of these community
workarounds substitutes for a regulated product and clinical oversight.
Third, a cautionary case in point. Melanotan II — an injectable
α-melanocyte-stimulating-hormone (melanocortin) analog sold illicitly
online as a "tanning" peptide and informal libido aid — is unapproved by the
FDA and by regulators in the UK, EU, and Australia, and it concentrates the
RUO hazards in one molecule. Acutely it causes nausea, facial flushing,
yawning and stretching, and appetite loss, and — through central
melanocortin receptors — spontaneous erections and priapism, the last a
urologic emergency that can require cavernosal aspiration or surgery.
Dermatologically it is the opposite of benign: by driving melanogenesis
indiscriminately it darkens and enlarges existing moles, provokes eruptive
and atypical (dysplastic) nevi and melanonychia, and has been linked in case
reports to melanoma arising in pre-existing lesions — causation is unproven,
but the mechanism (proliferation of melanocytes, including neoplastic
clones) makes it a real concern, and a personal or family history of melanoma
is an absolute contraindication. Serious systemic toxicity (rhabdomyolysis,
neurologic effects) and the usual non-sterile-injection risks round out a
profile with no place in a regenerative or aesthetic plan.[8]

Chapter 16 · The gut toolkit
Most gut "regeneration" runs through diet and the barrier — not a bottle
of peptides.
The highest-yield gut interventions are unglamorous and well evidenced.
Dietary fiber and minimally processed, plant-rich eating consistently improve
metabolic and inflammatory markers and feed
short-chain-fatty-acid–producing microbes that support the barrier GRADE
A.[1]
The incretin axis — now drugged with GLP-1 and GIP/GLP-1 agonists — is the
most powerful pharmacologic lever for the metabolic consequences of a
dysregulated gut–endocrine system (Chapter 12).[2]
Probiotics are widely used but the evidence is strain- and indication-specific,
not generic; benefits are modest and colonization is inconsistent GRADE
C.[3]
Next-generation organisms such as Akkermansia muciniphila show early
proof-of-concept for metabolic markers but remain exploratory GRADE C.[4]
The popular "leaky gut" repair stack — L-glutamine, zinc carnosine, and
peptides such as BPC-157 — has mechanistic and preclinical rationale for
barrier support, but human outcome data are limited, and BPC-157 carries
the regulatory constraints of Chapter 15 GRADE D.[5]

Chapter 17 · Supplements &
senotherapeutics
A small number of compounds have real human trials; most do not.
Grade accordingly.
The geroscience supplement market is vast and the human evidence shallow,
but a handful of compounds have reached randomized human trials — the
right place to focus.
Senolytics (dasatinib plus quercetin; fisetin) clear senescent cells and extend
healthspan in mice;[1] first-in-human pilots in pulmonary fibrosis and
diabetic kidney disease show feasibility and reduced senescent-cell burden
but are small, surrogate-only, and not outcome-powered, so any human
healthspan claim remains GRADE C.[2,3] Urolithin A, a mitophagy activator, is
safe and improved muscle strength and endurance in randomized trials
GRADE C — a muscle-function endpoint, not a proven healthspan
benefit.[4,5] Spermidine induces autophagy and is cardioprotective and
lifespan-extending in models, with supportive epidemiology GRADE C.[6]
NAD⁺ precursors (NR, NMN) reliably raise NAD⁺ and are well tolerated, but
durable outcome benefit is unproven GRADE C.[7]
Among the "boring" supplements, the evidence is paradoxically stronger for
endpoints that matter. Vitamin D did not reduce cancer or cardiovascular
events in the large VITAL trial, tempering enthusiasm for universal high-dose
use GRADE A — correct deficiency rather than chase a longevity effect.[8]
Omega-3 and adequate dietary protein support muscle and metabolic health
and belong in most regenerative plans.
The clinical posture follows from the evidence. Build the foundation from
what has human outcome data — protein, omega-3, corrected vitamin D, and
the lifestyle levers of the next chapter — then add at most one or two
mechanistically plausible agents tied to a defined endpoint, such as urolithin
A for muscle endurance, rather than stacking a dozen unproven compounds.
Each addition should earn its place: a reason to start, a target to watch, a trial
period, and an honest review of whether anything measurably changed. Most
of the market fails that test, and the opportunity cost is real — cost, pill
burden, and the false reassurance that displaces interventions that actually
work.

Chapter 18 · Lifestyle & circadian
optimization
The interventions with the strongest evidence cost the least and carry no
Category-2 label.
If regenerative endocrinology has a foundation, it is here. The lifestyle levers
outperform every supplement and most drugs for healthspan, and they
amplify whatever else is added.
Exercise is the closest thing to a polypill: a combination of resistance training
(to preserve muscle, bone, and insulin sensitivity) and aerobic conditioning
improves nearly every hallmark of aging GRADE A.[1]
Nutrition matters most as a pattern: a Mediterranean-style, minimally
processed, protein-adequate diet has randomized cardiovascular-prevention
evidence GRADE A,[2] and meal timing aligned to the active phase improves
insulin sensitivity independent of weight loss.[3]
Sleep is non-negotiable endocrine medicine: both short and long sleep
associate with higher mortality, and most GH secretion and tissue repair are
sleep-dependent GRADE B.[4] Protecting it starts with light hygiene — bright
morning light, dim evening light — given how readily evening light suppresses
melatonin.[5] Chronic psychological stress, via sustained cortisol, opposes all
of the above; stress regulation is a legitimate endocrine intervention.

Chapter 19 · Regenerative therapies
for endocrine organ & tissue
replacement
The horizon of the field: rebuilding the gland itself rather than replacing
its output.
Everything to this point has aimed at optimizing tissue that still functions.
Regenerative endocrinology in its most ambitious sense aims further — to
repair, replace, or regenerate the endocrine tissue itself and restore native,
regulated hormone production rather than approximating it with exogenous
replacement.[1,2] A transplanted, glucose-responsive beta-cell mass is a
categorically different thing from injected insulin; the promise is closed-loop
physiology, not open-loop dosing. Most of this work is investigational, but
one application has now reached early-phase (phase 1–2) human trials, so the
category can no longer be dismissed as speculative.[3]
T H E T O O L K I T
Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are adult somatic cells — a skin or
blood sample — reprogrammed to an embryonic-like state with defined
factors,[4] then differentiated toward a target endocrine lineage.[5]
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are adult cells used less to become the
missing tissue than to support it: they secrete growth factors, dampen
inflammation, and promote endogenous repair — a paracrine, not a

The honest summary: immense promise, narrow delivery. Most regenerative
endocrine therapies remain in research or trial phases, and translation faces
scientific and policy barriers alike.[12] For current status, the ISSCR's
guidelines for stem-cell research and clinical translation and
ClinicalTrials.gov are the right references[13] — and the right thing to give a

For the practitioner, the near-term reality is narrower than the headlines.
The first durable wins are arriving where the target is a single, well-defined
cell type with a clear functional readout — pancreatic beta cells for type 1
diabetes — while multicellular organs with intricate architecture and
feedback loops remain far from the clinic. That gap is precisely where
unregulated "stem-cell" and "regenerative" clinics market unproven infusions
for everything from anti-aging to hormonal decline, often at high cost and
real risk. The defensible stance is to follow the registered trials, use approved
products only within their narrow indications, and steer patients away from
cash-pay clinics selling a regeneration the evidence has not yet delivered.

Chapter 20 · Putting it together
A responsible framework for the regenerative-endocrinology consult.
The patient who comes in asking about peptides, hormones, and
"regeneration" is asking a reasonable question inside a field crowded with
hype. The clinician's job is to separate the levers that work from the ones that
merely sell — and to sequence them honestly.
A S A N O R D E R O F O P E R A T I O N S
1.
Build the foundation first. Exercise, nutrition, sleep, and circadian
alignment are the only interventions with grade-A healthspan evidence,
and they amplify everything layered on top. No peptide rescues a patient
who does not sleep.
2.
Correct what is measurably deficient. Replace hormones to a physiologic
target when there is a genuine deficiency and a guideline to support it —
testosterone in diagnosed hypogonadism,[1] menopausal hormone
therapy where indicated and not contraindicated.[2]
3.
Add evidence-graded adjuncts. A short list — urolithin A, corrected
vitamin D, GLP-1 agonists where indicated — has real human data.
4.
Treat everything else as investigational.
CLINICAL PEARL · THE REGULATORY LINE — Many popular regenerative
peptides — BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, epitalon among them
— were placed by the FDA into the 503A Category-2 list, leaving no clear
lawful pathway to compound them for human use; as of mid-2026 this is
under active reconsideration (a PCAC meeting in July 2026 is weighing
seven docketed peptides — BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, DSIP, semax,
and epitalon — for the bulks list; CJC-1295 and ipamorelin are not in that
round).[3] "Research use only" is not a clinical pathway. Document that
the patient understands a compound's regulatory status before any
prescription, and do not present unapproved peptides as established
protocols.
W H A T T O P R O M I S E , A N D W H A T N O T T O
Promise rigor, not reversal. The defensible message is that aging is modifiable
at the margins by well-evidenced means, that hormone optimization is
replacement medicine with rules, and that the frontier — cell replacement,
partial reprogramming — is genuinely exciting and genuinely not ready.[4] A
clinician who holds that line builds the one thing this field is short on: trust.

Clinical pearls
PEARL 1 — Foundation before frontier. Exercise, nutrition, sleep, and
circadian alignment are the only grade-A healthspan levers and they
amplify everything else. No peptide compensates for a patient who does
not sleep or train.
PEARL 2 — Replace deficiency, do not chase youth. Hormone therapy is
replacement medicine with rules — testosterone for diagnosed
hypogonadism, MHT where indicated. Treat to a physiologic target
against a deficiency, not to a "younger" number.
PEARL 3 — Evidence grade and legal status are different axes. A
compound can be popular, plausible, and still neither approved nor
lawfully compounded. Check both before prescribing, and document the
conversation.
PEARL 4 — Most peptides are grade C. BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295,
ipamorelin, epitalon and similar agents have thin human data, and their
FDA 503A status is unsettled: the seven docketed peptides (BPC-157, KPV,

— CJC-1295 and ipamorelin are not in that round — and review is not
approval. Present them as investigational, never as protocols.
PEARL 5 — The skin is an endocrine organ. It synthesizes and responds to
steroids, vitamin D, and neuropeptides. Estrogen loss drives much of the
collagen and hydration decline of menopausal skin — a useful counseling
frame.
PEARL 6 — The gut sets the endocrine tone. The microbiome regulates
GLP-1, the estrobolome, and systemic inflammation. Fiber and
food-pattern change outperform most probiotic products, whose effects
are strain-specific.

H O R M O N A L Y P R E S S
Inherited endocrine syndromes &
the skin
Cutaneous clues to genetic endocrinopathies — and the oncogene and
cyclic-AMP pathways behind them.
Many familial endocrinopathies announce themselves on the skin before the
gland declares itself, and a few signs are close to pathognomonic. Reading
them turns a cosmetic complaint into an early diagnosis — and a referral.[1]
The mechanisms are not random: most cluster around two molecular themes
that this chapter uses as its organizing logic.
T W O M O L E C U L A R T H E M E S
The first is the tumor-suppressor / oncogene axis. Loss of a suppressor
(menin in MEN1, neurofibromin in NF1, PTEN in Cowden) or activation of an
oncogene (the RET receptor tyrosine kinase in MEN2) drives the benign skin
tumors — angiofibromas, neurofibromas, trichilemmomas — that flag the
syndrome. The second is the cyclic-AMP–protein-kinase-A (PKA) axis.
Because cAMP/PKA signaling governs both melanocyte and endocrine-cell
behavior, variants that disinhibit it produce a characteristic pairing of
pigmented skin lesions with hormone excess. These two themes recur below.
M U L T I P L E E N D O C R I N E N E O P L A S I A
MEN1 (Wermer syndrome) follows loss of the tumor suppressor menin. Its
cutaneous signature is striking and underused diagnostically: multiple facial
angiofibromas (~88% of patients), truncal collagenomas (~72%), and lipomas,
often years before the parathyroid, pituitary, or pancreatic tumors are
found.[2] These angiofibromas are indistinguishable from those of tuberous
sclerosis (a TSC1/TSC2–mTOR disorder), so it is the company they keep —
the endocrine tumors — that points to MEN1. MEN2A (Sipple syndrome) is
the oncogene mirror — a gain-of-function variant in the RET receptor
tyrosine kinase causing medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytoma,
and parathyroid disease. Its skin clue is cutaneous lichen amyloidosis:
pruritic, rippled, hyperpigmented plaques over the upper back and
interscapular region that cluster with RET codon-634 variants — in effect a
visible marker of medullary-thyroid-cancer risk.[3] Its more aggressive
sibling, MEN2B, follows the RET M918T variant and is recognizable on exam
by mucosal neuromas of the lips and tongue and a marfanoid habitus, with
early, aggressive medullary thyroid carcinoma and pheochromocytoma but,
unlike MEN2A, no hyperparathyroidism.[3]

T H E C Y C L I C - A M P / P K A S Y N D R O M E S
Carney complex is the cleanest illustration of the cAMP theme. Inactivating
variants in PRKAR1A — the R1α regulatory subunit of PKA, found in roughly
70% of cases — release the PKA catalytic subunits from cAMP control, so the
kinase fires constitutively. The result is a distinctive skin picture (perioral and
eyelid lentigines, multiple blue nevi, and cutaneous myxomas) paired with
endocrine overactivity: ACTH-independent Cushing from primary pigmented
nodular adrenocortical disease (PPNAD), acromegaly, and thyroid tumors.[4]
The same axis explains two GNAS (Gsα) disorders at opposite poles:
McCune–Albright syndrome (a mosaic activating variant) gives large, jagged
“coast-of-Maine” cafe-au-lait macules with fibrous dysplasia and precocious
puberty, while pseudohypoparathyroidism (a loss-of-function variant causing
PTH resistance) produces Albright hereditary osteodystrophy — round face,
short stature, brachydactyly, and subcutaneous ossifications.
R A S A N D P T E N : N F 1 A N D C O W D E N
Neurofibromatosis type 1 stems from loss of neurofibromin, a negative
regulator of RAS; the cutaneous hallmarks — cafe-au-lait macules, dermal
neurofibromas, and axillary or inguinal freckling — carry an endocrine tail,
most importantly pheochromocytoma.[1] Cowden syndrome (PTEN loss) is
almost universally cutaneous — facial trichilemmomas, oral papillomatosis
(cobblestoning), and acral keratoses — and carries a high lifetime risk of
thyroid (often follicular) carcinoma and goiter.
A U T O I M M U N E P O L Y E N D O C R I N E S Y N D R O M E S
Where the syndromes above are neoplastic, the autoimmune polyendocrine
syndromes are failures of self-tolerance. APS-1 (APECED), caused by
recessive AIRE variants, presents early with chronic mucocutaneous
candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, and primary adrenal insufficiency, alongside
vitiligo, alopecia areata, and nail or enamel dystrophy. APS-2 and APS-3 are
polygenic and HLA-linked, pairing autoimmune thyroid disease and type 1
diabetes with the same dermatologic markers — vitiligo and alopecia
areata.[5]

The adrenal failure of APS is autoimmune, but primary adrenal insufficiency
is just as often inherited — congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase
deficiency, CYP21A2), X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (ABCD1), and the

loss of cortisol feedback drives POMC-derived ACTH and α-MSH, so
melanocortin signaling rises and the patient develops hyperpigmentation —
darkest in palmar creases, scars, and mucosa — with congenital adrenal
hyperplasia adding androgen-driven hirsutism and acne.[1] A second
metabolic-skin pairing is familial partial lipodystrophy (LMNA, PPARG):
selective loss of limb and gluteal subcutaneous fat gives a deceptively
muscular build, while the redirected metabolic load produces severe insulin
resistance, early diabetes, and florid acanthosis nigricans — the same
cutaneous readout of hyperinsulinemia seen in the receptor defects below.[1]
